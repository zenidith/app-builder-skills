# Notes: Write to Sell: The Ultimate Guide to Great Copywriting (Andy Maslen, 2009)

Paraphrased technique notes for internal use, written in our own words; not the book's text. Page numbers are PDF page indices of the source file.

Book shape: six sections. One: the reader (Ch 1-5). Two: planning (Ch 6-10). Three: what works and what doesn't (Ch 11-12). Four: writing style (Ch 13-18). Five: editing, proofing, readability, layout, design (Ch 19-23). Six: next steps (Ch 24-26), plus a web appendix.

## Introduction (pp. 8-13)

- Defines selling broadly: getting someone to do, feel or think something they weren't doing, feeling or thinking before. That covers internal pitches, partner deals and favours as well as orders and leads (p. 8).
- Four things to always remember (pp. 10-11): (1) copywriting is selling first and writing second, so it needs an understanding of how people think; (2) put the reader at the centre, not the company, product or writer; (3) small is beautiful: short words and short sentences engage even senior readers, while long ones are "all head, no heart"; (4) most people who write sales copy were never trained in selling or in writing.
- Use plain, earthy, concrete words, which the author calls "wheelbarrow" language, rather than abstract corporate vocabulary.

## Ch 1. Where most people go wrong (pp. 17-18)

- Write what the reader wants to read, not what you want to say. Most copy is a list of the points the writer wants to get across.
- Unlike a friend's letter or a magazine they paid for, a sales message gets no goodwill from the reader. They have invested nothing in it and will drop it at the first dull line.
- Good writing should be so smooth that the reader notices the content, not the craft.
- The reader is tuned to "Radio WIIFM" (What's In It For Me), not your "WIII" (What I'm Interested In). Lose them and they switch stations.

## Ch 2. A few thoughts on human nature (pp. 20-23)

- At any moment the reader is thinking about themselves, not you. Copy that opens with the writer's feelings ("we're delighted to announce"), the company's history or claims about its own job gets ignored.
- Profile the reader with questions: sex, age, what they want more of and less of, where they'd rather be, what they value, how they see themselves and how they want others to see them, whether they decide with head or heart, and whether they want gain or relief from worry.
- Tell them what the product will do for them: make life easier, better or more rewarding.
- The seven deadly sins are a map of motives: pride (flattery, status), envy (others already have it), gluttony (comfort, indulgence), lust (desire), anger (a way out, a rival's failings), greed (more for less), sloth (less effort). Use them subtly.
- People buy on emotion and justify with logic, so give the emotional pull and also the objective reasons they can quote to themselves or a boss.

## Ch 3. Getting to know your reader (pp. 25-32)

- A "Dear Customer" mindset fails. Write to one real person.
- The "3am question": what keeps this reader awake at night? Start there.
- Go to the "engine room": talk to customers where they are (shops, call centres, events), listen to sales staff and front-line people, and ask real buyers why they bought.
- Write what you would say face to face. Keep a sketch or photo of a typical reader by your screen.
- Build an attribute table. For each reader trait (busy, tired, ambitious) note what they want to move away from (stress, low status) and toward (time, sleep, money, power). For every "more" there is usually a matching "less".
- Fear and greed are the two basic levers in the toolkit.
- With several audiences, merge them into one typical reader who carries the shared traits. Don't write "some of you" or "many of you". Use "you", or "there are people who..." when you need a wider group.

## Ch 4. A word about businesspeople (pp. 34-36)

- Companies don't buy; people do. A four-part view of the B2B reader:
  - At work they are busy, so be relevant rather than merely brief.
  - They are people, with personal motives such as bonus, promotion, looking good and avoiding blame.
  - Sell business benefits: saved money or time, peace of mind, profit, productivity. Where a group decides, address each member's concern.
  - Write in plain English. Swap jargon like "prior to" for "before" and drop clichés like "going forward".

## Ch 5. The B-word (and why it's so much better than the F-word) (pp. 38-39)

- Benefits beat features. Makers fall in love with features, but buyers want to know why a thing matters to them, not what it is.
- Meet a felt need and you make the sale.
- Summary rule: benefits make the sale; features let the buyer justify it.

## Ch 6. Yes, but what are you trying to say? (pp. 43-46)

- If you can't say it out loud simply, you aren't ready to write it.
- Six planning questions: What do I want to achieve? Who is the reader and what do they know? What do I need to say (facts, plus the reader's needs)? How much space do I have? How should it come across (tone)? When is it due?
- Plan away from the screen, on paper, in key words and bullets rather than sentences. Sticky notes help you group points into themes (for a proposal: benefits to the reader, capability, track record, costs, background).
- Fit the plan on one sheet: the goal at the top, then sections in a logical order with a clear beginning, middle and end.

## Ch 7. Setting out your goals (pp. 48-49)

- The job of copy is to do what your best salesperson would do in person.
- Pick a concrete commercial goal: a sale, a trial, an opt-in, a sales meeting, a new account, a bigger spend, a renewal, a visit.
- Being creative is not a goal. If a line mainly pleases you or impresses colleagues, cut it ("murder your darlings").
- Make goals SMART. "Raise brand awareness" and similar vanity goals don't count.

## Ch 8. A simple but powerful fast-food mnemonic for creating perfect plans (pp. 51-52)

- KFC: what the reader should Know, Feel and do (Commit, the call to action).
  - Know: the facts they need to justify the decision.
  - Feel: the emotion that moves them (excited, worried about missing out, reassured, envious).
  - Commit: exactly what to do, how, and by when.
- Most copy leaves out Feel. Stories, pictures and involving the reader supply it.

## Ch 9. Understanding what you're really selling (pp. 54-57)

- Sell what the product does, not what it is. Turn facts into dramatised benefits.
- Exercise: what it appears to be, what it appears to compete with, what it really is, what it really competes with. A luxury pen is a status gift that competes with watches. A lottery ticket is cheap escapism that competes with chocolate.
- Show, don't tell. Open with "picture the scene" (p. 56) and paint the reader's life with the product, and without it.
- Consequences grid: what will and won't happen if they do act, and if they don't. Fill all four boxes and use the strongest lines (pp. 56-57).

## Ch 10. The killer structure for every piece of copy you'll ever write, online or offline (pp. 59-76)

- AIDCA: Attention, Interest, Desire, Conviction, Action. It applies to letters, emails, web pages and ads.
- Attention: the headline, subject line or envelope does this job. Many more people read the headline than the body (about four times as many, p. 60). In tests, benefit headlines beat news and curiosity headlines, and a mix of all three is strongest. Puzzle or clever headlines fail because the reader neither knows nor cares.
- Write a dozen or more headlines, then pick or combine. Use a placeholder while drafting. Never end a headline with a full stop, because it signals "stop reading" (p. 61).
- Headline length: as long as it needs, roughly 10-14 words or one line, and shorter is usually better. Quick starters are "How..." and "Now..." (p. 62).
- Images: show the product in use. Avoid generic smiling businesspeople, animals and glamour shots. Use professional photos (p. 63).
- Interest: tie the product to the reader's problem as its solution and write about the solution more than the problem. Answer "how will my life improve?" in the first sentence or paragraph (p. 63).
- Feature, advantage, benefit chain: a feature gives an advantage, which delivers a benefit. Ask "so what?" of each claim until you reach something the reader values. A two-column feature-to-benefit list helps (pp. 63-66).
- NIB: benefits can be Noble (motives people will admit to), Immediate (free delivery, instant access) or Basic (the real motive, such as ego). Cover all three (p. 66).
- Be specific. An exact figure ("an extra $1,000 a month") beats a vague promise. The book lists common benefit types: make money, save time, gain respect, peace of mind, a dream holiday and so on (pp. 66-68).
- Desire: people want only some of the things that interest them, and once they want something they will find the need. Make the product feel both high-need and high-want. Let the reader star in a story of life with it, and stress that the problem lasts only as long as they go without (pp. 68-70).
- Scarcity: limit supply by time (a deadline or limited-time price) or by quantity (a fixed number of places) to trigger fear of missing out. Other desire drivers: being specially selected, being first, respected people already own it, only you supply it, a rival will get it, it beats alternatives, acting is easy (pp. 70-71).
- Conviction: show that the risk of buying is smaller than the risk of not buying. Testimonials must carry full attribution (name, place, detail), and a specific story beats a generic compliment (p. 72).
- Collecting testimonials: ask your best and repeat customers; draft something in their own voice for them to approve (offer a choice); or phone them to capture natural speech; don't over-polish; give quotes visual prominence (pp. 73-74).
- Other convincers: free samples, statistics, free trials, press coverage, third-party endorsements, money-back guarantees. Use as many as you can (p. 74).
- Action: repeat the call to action throughout and put a link near the top of an email. Keep it short, simple, direct and clear. Use the imperative with a deadline ("Order by 15 January"), not "if you'd like to...". Offer several ways to respond. Write the call to action first, working back from the end (pp. 74-75).

## Ch 11. What works (pp. 80-89)

- Reader focus: the opening sentence is the hook, so talk about the reader. Imagine them sitting opposite you and judge their reaction to each sentence (pp. 80-81).
- Brevity: cut padding such as "background" sections that serve the writer (pp. 81-82).
- Long copy often beats short copy in direct-response tests, as long as it stays interesting. Interest matters more than length, so test (pp. 82-83).
- Storytelling: characters, a predicament and dialogue, kept relevant to the reader. A case study is a story. "Like you, [name]..." openings put the reader inside it (pp. 83-84).
- Questions: readers answer them automatically. Closed questions help close, limited questions offer a choice of two, and open questions build connection. A run of three sure-yes questions followed by the solution is a classic opener (pp. 84-86).
- Rapport: use research to name their worries so they nod along. "As you know..." makes the reader feel informed. Set the register by the reader's relationship with the brand, not by their education (pp. 86-87).
- Fresh ideas, about 20 tips: walk or exercise, free-write, use pen and paper, use the product yourself, draw the buyer, read poetry, quotes and other people's copy, script a conversation between a buyer and a non-buyer, ask friends what would make them buy, keep an ideas book, change where you work (pp. 88-89).

## Ch 12. And what doesn't (pp. 91-100)

- Showy writing: copy is a window, and the reader should see the view, not the glass. Laboured analogies (cheetah-speed ads) are the classic sin (p. 91).
- Jargon signals status, not meaning. Keep only the technical terms the reader truly uses (p. 92).
- Wordiness: long copy can work, but long and boring can't. Swap wordy phrases for plain ones and cut redundant adjectives, repetition, clichés, tautology, abstract nouns, superlatives and waffle (pp. 92-94).
- Talking about yourself: drop "delighted to write" openers and company history at the top. Credentials belong later and in brief, as Conviction (pp. 94-95).
- Boilerplate ("as a valued client", "please don't hesitate to contact me") reads as automatic. Pasted blocks cause jolts in tone (pp. 95-97).
- Over-excitement: words like exciting, amazing, unique and revolutionary tell the reader what to feel without making them feel it. Ask why it is exciting and write that instead (pp. 97-98).
- Humour mostly distracts. The reader smiles and doesn't buy. Witty brand ads work only for huge brands with a clear benefit underneath (pp. 98-99).
- Subjectivity: approvers judge copy on "do I like it" rather than "will it work". Judge on results and tests (one test found a typewriter-style font beat standard fonts) (pp. 99-100).

## Ch 13. The black arts (and a few magic words) (pp. 104-108)

- Read-on devices: split a list across paragraphs; end a paragraph on a teaser ("and here's why..."); break a page mid-sentence; promise something good later ("in a moment I'll show you..."); give a specific reason to turn the page or scroll ("three more reasons to book" beats "see over").
- Magic words:
  - "Easy": people avoid effort, so say "three easy steps".
  - "Quick": sell the lawn, not the grass seed, and add a timeframe.
  - "Free": don't inflate it to "absolutely free"; state the gift's value; check spam filters in email.
  - "Now": signals newness, urgency, or the shift from problem to solution ("It used to be... Now you can").
  - "Please": courtesy.
  - "Guarantee": removes fear, because the reader hears "this will work".

## Ch 14. Why your sales copy should be like a bowl of Rice Krispies (pp. 110-116)

- Five ways to give copy snap:
  - Power words: short, vivid, often one-syllable words carry meaning plus a sensory jolt. Upgrade weak words: "cost-effective" to "cheap" or "good value", "optimal" to "best", "impact negatively" to "hurt", "reliable" to "rock-solid".
  - Personal words: "you" above all. "We" and "I" beat the company name. Aim for about three "you"s to every "I" or "we".
  - Verbs, not nouns: cure "nounitis" by removing -tion, -ment and -ance constructions. "We solve IT problems" beats "our specialization is the provision of IT solutions" and is shorter.
  - Anglo-Saxon over Latinate: before, not prior to; get, not obtain; end, not terminate. This holds for senior readers too, who are busier.
  - Positive over negative: "hurry" beats "don't delay", "remember" beats "don't forget", and lead a guarantee with satisfaction rather than the refund.

## Ch 15. Good English style for copywriting (pp. 118-129)

- Takes Orwell's six rules as the base.
- Clichés tell the reader "I couldn't be bothered". Watch everyday clichés, business clichés ("low-hanging fruit", "going forward"), in-house phrases and mixed metaphors (pp. 118-120).
- Short words: avoid "thesaurusitis". Rewrite inflated phrases as plain actions, e.g. "find out what works best, then share it" (pp. 120-121).
- Cut words (pp. 121-124): use a precise noun or verb instead of adjective plus noun ("mansion", not "very big house"); use adjectives only to add information, not emphasis; cut redundant phrases ("at this point in time" becomes "now", "due to the fact that" becomes "because"); replace abstract-noun phrases ("on a quarterly basis" becomes "quarterly").
- Active voice: shorter (passive can add about a third to length), more personal, and it takes responsibility ("I'm sorry we..."). Find passives by searching for "by" and "to be" plus a past participle, and aim for close to zero (pp. 124-126).
- Swap foreign phrases and jargon for plain English. Break a rule rather than write something ugly: judge by sound and effect (pp. 126-127).
- Sentence length: average about 16 words, mixing short and longer sentences. Sentences of 12 words or fewer are almost always understood; rework anything in the 20s or 30s (pp. 127-128).
- Hot spot: the last word of a sentence lingers, so end on the power word (p. 128).

## Ch 16. Tone of voice: what it is and what to do with it (pp. 131-133)

- Write as you'd speak, then adjust formality when editing. Read it aloud: would you say this to the customer's face or on the phone?
- Grammatical moods set the tone:
  - Indicative: plain fact, neutral.
  - Interrogative: a question, which opens a conversation.
  - Conditional: a bargain or win-win ("if you..., we'll...").
  - Imperative: a command, authoritative, used for calls to action.
  - Subjunctive: a hypothetical, for exploring.
- Use contractions for a conversational tone. Drop courtroom words like "hence", "thus" and "indeed".

## Ch 17. Why punctuation is a sales tool (pp. 135-146)

- Punctuation exists to make meaning clear, not to mark breaths. Bad punctuation can reverse meaning.
- Commas: set off extra information, with one comma at the start or end of a sentence and a pair in the middle. Don't add a dramatic comma before a final word.
- Semicolons: join closely linked sentences, or separate long list items that contain commas.
- Colons: introduce a list; build tension by putting the list first ("Light, strong, cheaper: three reasons to switch"); set up a promise and its payoff; add punch. No colon after "including".
- Full stops: the best friend of readable copy, so use them early and often. A new subject needs a new sentence (no comma splices). Prefer full stops to semicolons and colons.
- Exclamation marks are like canned laughter: hype and spam signals. No scare quotes around metaphors or jargon.
- Apostrophes: get contractions and possessives right (no greengrocer's apostrophes). Ellipsis: for omitted words, a pause, or a cliffhanger.

## Ch 18. Stuff you learned at school you can forget (pp. 148-152)

- Starting a sentence with And or But, ending on a preposition and splitting an infinitive are fine. These "rules" were borrowed from Latin. Trust your ear and clarity, but don't needlessly upset a pedantic reader.
- Section summary: the reader is looking for a reason to stop, so give them none.

## Ch 19. Editing your work (pp. 156-158)

- A first draft is never finished. Overwrite first, then cut anything that doesn't serve the stated goal. Length is as much as needed, then stop.
- Check the first draft with ten questions: is it in the reader's language, does it follow the plan and purpose, and is it clear, concise, interesting, unambiguous, well laid out, free of waffle and clichés, in the right order, with graphics that earn their place?
- Do at least three drafts, ideally five:
  1. Get it down.
  2. Check it against the plan.
  3. Fix structure and cut sections and words.
  4. Polish tone, metaphors, style and punctuation.
  5. Print, spellcheck and proofread.
- The tools get finer each time: chainsaw, hedge trimmer, shears, scissors, scalpel. Know when returns diminish, and beware too many approvers.

## Ch 20. Proof-reading (pp. 160-163)

- A misspelt client or customer name tells them you don't respect them.
- Proof on paper, after a gap (a day is ideal, an hour at least), or use a proofing partner.
- Check one thing at a time (spelling, then grammar, then punctuation, then word choice, then spacing), or one layer at a time (headings, subheads, captions, links, body).
- Spellcheckers miss real-word errors such as "form" for "from". Punctuation errors are worse than spelling errors because they can change meaning.
- Word-by-word method: read backwards, bottom to top, right to left, masking the text you've already read.

## Ch 21. Checking readability (pp. 165-167)

- Run readability statistics: words per sentence, percentage of passive sentences, Flesch Reading Ease (higher is easier; about 60 or above is plain English) and Flesch-Kincaid grade (about grade 7, a reading age of roughly 12).
- Readers with no stake in your message read as if at a lower level. Below about 50 Reading Ease, shorten and split sentences and swap long words for short ones ("help", not "assistance").

## Ch 22. Layout: what you can do to help your reader (pp. 169-176)

- Signpost: a contents list for anything over about 8 pages, a consistent heading hierarchy, colour-coding and arrows. Flashes and starbursts get read first.
- Every graphic needs a purpose. Avoid clichés (clocks, cogs, chess pieces, handshakes, cheetahs). Captions are read straight after the picture, so use them to sell.
- Type: body at least 10-11 pt; serif for printed body text, sans-serif for headlines and screens. Range text left with a ragged right edge. Avoid justified, centred, right-aligned, reversed-out and shaped text. Don't split a bullet list across columns.
- Paragraphs: break them after about 5-6 lines in print or 2-3 lines on the web. End a paragraph on an open question to pull the reader on.
- Contrast: black on white is best. Never use pale grey or yellow on white, or red on green.

## Ch 23. Design: what designers can do (and what you should never allow them to) (pp. 178-181)

- A good designer reads the copy and pulls out key lines as heads and quotes. Good design goes unnoticed. Give the designer a written brief.
- Three sins to block: response forms that are hard to fill in (boxes too small, more than two cuts to a coupon), poor typography (tiny type, low contrast, text over busy photos), and meaningless imagery. Show what the buyer will actually get, ideally with original photography.

## Ch 24. My tips for action (pp. 185-187)

- Read widely, including punchy tabloid English and the direct-response classics (Ogilvy, Bird, Hopkins, Bly). Keep good phrases.
- Practise deliberately: print your copy, leave it overnight and critique it. Shorten recent pieces. Swap work with a writing buddy. Copy great ads out by hand. Re-check readability at every draft.

## Ch 25. How to brief an external copywriter (pp. 189-192)

- A brief says who you are, what the product is, who the customers are and what you want to achieve. It doesn't say how. It is the written record against which the copy is judged.
- What a brief covers: the reader (their 3am worry, what they want more and less of, the consequence questions); objectives in Know, Feel and Commit terms; the product; its unique selling point and its weaknesses; the main benefit; strategy and channels; lists and media; schedule; brand values and positioning; design needs.
- When giving corrections, start with what works. Separate the writer's misunderstanding (fixed free) from your own change of mind (paid).

## Ch 26. The last word (pp. 194-195)

- Good writing always outperforms. Treat the language with respect.

## Appendix 1. Writing for the web: myths and realities (pp. 197-201)

- The web changes little. The reader still has to be persuaded to open, read, believe and act, and AIDCA still applies.
- Myths the author rejects: that web copy must be information rather than promotion, that only the web gets scanned, that it must always be ultra-simple, that local idiom is bad.
- Search results work as headlines. Never use "Welcome to..." as a headline; use a benefit, news or curiosity instead. Even navigation labels can sell: a benefits-led link label beats a bare "Benefits".
- Email subject lines: put the strongest keyword first, avoid spam-trigger wording, and keep to about 40 characters including spaces so they aren't truncated.
- Online sentences: aim for about 10 words on average and a conversational, more relaxed register that still fits the brand.
- Length: the "web copy must be short" claim is unproven. Keep it relevant and test lengths.
- Use links to layer the story: a quick read on top and deeper detail one click down for the curious or sceptical. Online copy must still sell, and check readability ruthlessly.

## How to apply
(Invented illustrations across different kinds of business.)
- Plan on one page before writing: goal (e.g. trial sign-ups), reader (a busy clinic manager), Know/Feel/Commit, the one promise, the objections.
- Features into benefits with "so what?": a standing desk's "electric motor, 3 memory presets" becomes "switch from sitting to standing in one tap, at exactly your height".
- AIDCA on a product page for a language-learning app: attention (a headline with the outcome), interest (the reader's stalled progress), desire (10-minute daily lessons, real conversations), conviction (reviews, a free first week), action (one button).
- You-focus: rewrite "We are a family-run bakery established in 1998" into "Bread baked at 5am, on your table by breakfast".
- Real scarcity only: a ceramics studio's limited kiln batch or a workshop's real seat count can carry an honest deadline; never an invented countdown.
- Editing pass for all copy: cut hype (amazing, unforgettable, unique) unless the reason follows straight away, check readability (Reading Ease 60 or higher, average sentence 10–16 words), active voice, and proofread names and numbers separately.
- Images: the book's warning against generic stock imagery supports using real photos of the product, the people and the place, with captions that sell.
