# Notes: The Copywriting Sourcebook (Andy Maslen, 2010)

Paraphrased technique notes for the shared copywriting skill, written in our own words. This is not the book's text. Page numbers are the printed page numbers of the 2010 edition (ebook copy); where a point spans a chapter, only the chapter range is given.

The book is organised by format: each chapter covers what the piece is for, strengths and weaknesses, what goes wrong, what to include and leave out, structure, tone, and images. The recurring spine across all formats: lead with the reader's benefit, address one reader as "you", use plain short words, prove every claim, answer objections, and end with one clear call to action.

## Ch 1. Headline (pp. 5–12)
- A headline's real jobs: stop the reader, make them want to read the body, and compress the sales pitch into roughly 10–16 words. Showing off, wordplay, matching the picture or "brand awareness" are not jobs (p. 6).
- Three basic types: news, curiosity, benefit. Benefit tends to beat news, which beats curiosity; a headline that combines all three is strongest (p. 6–7).
- Emergency method when stuck (p. 7): complete the sentence "My product helps my customers because it…", delete everything up to and including "it", switch "they/them" to "you", and optionally prefix "NOW: an easy way to". You get a plain benefit headline in a minute.
- "How to" headlines reliably work; "NOW:" injects news; FREE still pulls, in business markets too (p. 8).
- Length: under 16 words is good, around 10 is better. Use everyday words (prefer "talking" to "communication"). One line ideally, two at most; overflow goes into a subhead (p. 8–9).
- A call-out line above the headline can select the target reader ("Attention all home bakers…") before the benefit headline (p. 9).
- Idea bank (p. 9–10): launch words (Introducing, Announcing, At last, New); journalistic "secrets revealed" and big-number lists; open a story; compare value with price; break the price into a small per-day or per-month figure; lead with the discount; practical steps ("Seven steps to…", "Do you make these mistakes…?"); a customer quote as the headline; a question; starting mid-thought ("Because…").
- Final checks: no puns or teasers, especially online where people scan; spell out the main benefit; apply the "so what?" test to the draft (p. 11).

## Ch 2. Sales letter (pp. 13–28)
- A letter must look like a letter: personally addressed, signed by a real person, plain typeface, minimal design, "you" throughout, and one single goal pursued relentlessly (pp. 13–14).
- Classic failures (pp. 15–21): opening with your own feelings ("we're delighted…"); opening with a lecture on the market; stock phrases like "as a valued customer"; "Dear Customer" when you have the name; paragraphs over about five lines; speaking to a group ("some of you") instead of one person; listing features with no benefits; glitzy design; too-small or fancy type; no cross-heads; copy so short it leaves out persuasive points; signing as "the team"; skipping the PS.
- Feature-to-benefit drill (p. 18): for each feature ask "what's in it for me?", then keep asking "so what?" until the answer is something the reader cares about (stronger metal → sturdier gate → child stays safe).
- Must include (pp. 21–22): every feature turned into a benefit; genuine, unpolished testimonials; objections raised and answered (endorsements if you are new; price broken down per day or compared with the payoff; a money-back guarantee); and a strong call to action that restates the main benefit and the offer.
- Leave out: your first-draft opening paragraph (usually warm-up, delete it), industry generalisations, company history beyond a line (let logos, awards and guarantees do the reassuring), and any second offer or second call to action (pp. 21–23).
- Structure (pp. 23–25): headline (benefit, offer, curiosity); subhead taking a different angle such as the offer; personal salutation; first paragraph leads with the main benefit, a question or a short story; body expands, proves, adds benefits and evidence; testimonials in ones or threes, introduced as "don't take our word for it"; devices that keep pages turning; call to action as a clear instruction with a deadline and incentive; PS that restates benefit, offer and action (not small print).

## Ch 3. Case study (pp. 29–42)
- A case study is a true story of a client problem you solved. It shows rather than tells the benefit, and because it reads as information it disarms scepticism (pp. 29–31).
- Bring in the client's own voice and quotes; without it, it is just a boast in disguise. Tell it as a coherent story with a hero overcoming a challenge (pp. 33–34).
- Must include: hard figures, client profile (name, job title, industry) so readers identify, timeline, the problem, your relevant capabilities, what you did, the outcome, client quotes (pp. 35–36).
- Structure (pp. 35–37): headline naming the challenge; label it as a case study; a standfirst covering who, what, where, when, why; then challenge (benefit just out of reach, and how they chose you), expected benefits, what happened (people, dates, small setbacks, dialogue), outcome with figures and a quote, "next steps" showing the ongoing relationship, and a soft call to action with an incentive (consultation, health check, another case study).
- Tone: serious, authoritative, plain, past tense, active voice. Use real photos of the client, never stock, and use captions with quotes (pp. 34, 38).

## Ch 4. Press release (pp. 43–56)
- Write it as the journalist would for their readers; do not sell. Pick a genuinely newsworthy subject and match the outlet's style (pp. 43–45).
- Failures (pp. 46–50): not relevant to that outlet (write different versions with different hooks per media sector); a subject line that just says "press release"; "delighted to announce" headlines; slow starts (news first, company profile last); no actual news; selling language like "revolutionary", "unique" and "groundbreaking"; unsupported claims; jargon.
- Paragraph 1 answers who, what, where, when, why and how, plus a spark; paragraph 2 evidence; paragraph 3 a quote with name and title; paragraph 4 links to the company; then notes for editors (profile, contact, photo opportunity) (pp. 49–53).
- The headline should work as an email subject for an audience of one (the journalist). Angles that work: a survey finding, a list, a statistic, controversy, geography (p. 50–52).

## Ch 5. Product brochure (pp. 57–72)
- Even an emotional purchase needs facts to satisfy the reader's "inner finance director": specs, performance, options, cost, delivery, guarantees, what happens if it goes wrong (pp. 61–64).
- Leave out scene-setting history, self-indulgent mood writing and abstract aspirations; favour facts over feelings (p. 65).
- Useful devices: bulleted lists (numbered only when order matters, no semicolons); a Q&A section where you choose questions that lead to selling answers; specific testimonials; a "who should buy this?" panel listing each role in the decision (user, specifier, approver, spouse) and what each wants (pp. 66–67).
- Call to action: label it "Order now", not "Order form", and have an outsider test the response route (p. 67–68).
- Images: show the product with callouts that state the feature and why it matters; dramatise the benefit; use original photography (pp. 69–71).

## Ch 6. Press ad (pp. 73–90)
- Failures: looking too much like an ad; too little copy to answer "what's in it for me?" and "what if it goes wrong?"; too much white space; picture leading the copy; no reason to act now (appeal to self-interest: save money, save effort, family, fear, pride); no call to action; me-too clichés (pp. 75–78).
- Include: how life gets easier in detail; answers to objections (why it costs more); evidence (stats, endorsements, case studies); reassuring testimonials; a picture of the product or the benefit; a call to action (pp. 78–79).
- Leave out: mission statements, describing what it is instead of what it does, and hype words such as exciting, fantastic, unique, revolutionary, amazing, incredible (p. 79–80).
- Structure: call-out line; headline of 10–16 words; subhead with the offer; body that appeals, expands, proves, reassures, builds desire and asks (pp. 80–81).
- The first sentence exists only to get the second one read; keep it very short. Raise and resolve the biggest objection early (pp. 81–82).
- Long copy usually outpulls short because only interested people buy and they want the detail; test it rather than assume (pp. 82–83).
- Reassurance tools: one to three testimonials, awards, press coverage, a money-back guarantee in a panel or seal. Call to action: imperative, deadline, incentive, simple response; offer contact channels but minimise choices, since fewer options lift response (pp. 83–84).
- Write for one reader alone, in plain English; nobody talks in corporate or B2B jargon. Images that work: the product, customers using it, people like the reader, the improved outcome; avoid stock clichés (handshakes, chess pieces, big cats) and images that literally illustrate a pun (pp. 84–87).

## Ch 7. Presentation (pp. 91–102)
- Plan before opening the software: one business goal, audience research, objectives for what they should know, feel and commit to, the product interrogated from the audience's side, then structure. Software comes last (p. 90–91).
- Treat it as three separate pieces: brief slides, speaker notes, and a separate leave-behind (pp. 96–97).
- Structure: why you are there; the problem and outline answer; why your answer is better; proof; a call to action. Start with the audience's own issues (pp. 97–98).
- The six-by-six rule: at most six bullets of six words; write slide text like newspaper headlines; no semicolons; one-line titles (p. 98).

## Ch 8. E-zine (pp. 103–118)
- An e-zine is a free, regular (at least monthly), genuinely useful newsletter with the feel of a personal letter, sent with permission. Because it is free and easy to delete it must over-deliver on value (pp. 103–105).
- Its commercial job is to be in front of customers when they are ready to buy; the content is information related to what you sell (gardening tips if you sell tools) (pp. 105–107).
- Failures: boring, badly written, impersonal, too much selling (keep promotion to about a quarter or less), too short (under about three screens) or a main article over about 700 words (pp. 107–109).
- Content ideas: how-to pieces, numbered top tips, mini case studies, interviews in Q&A form, research snippets (p. 110).
- Five zones: masthead, a personal publisher's note, an opinionated main article with facts readers won't find elsewhere, clearly marked promotions, housekeeping (pp. 111–113).
- Style: informal but authoritative, plain English, short paragraphs, section heads. Put the word count and reading time at the top. The subject line names the e-zine plus a flavour of the content, and the first lines visible in the preview pane must carry the value (a contents list helps) (pp. 114–115).

## Ch 9. Corporate brochure (pp. 119–136)
- Readers care about themselves, not your opinion of yourself; give it a commercial goal and decide what readers should do after reading (pp. 119–125).
- Leave out: empty adjectives (an adjective should add information, not emphasis), fad phrases, long history; swap "we" for "you" (pp. 128–129).
- Style: short words, sentences around 16 words, short Anglo-Saxon words over Latinate ones (let not permit, stop not terminate, before not prior to); ban "solutions" (p. 130).

## Ch 10. Email (pp. 137–154)
- One-to-one business email basics: polite opening and sign-off, spell the name right, get to the point in a friendly way, give a deadline when you want action, and think how tone or humour might be misread (p. 138).
- A promotional email is judged in under a second on the From line and subject line, then on what shows in the preview pane (p. 138–139).
- Failures (pp. 141–143): a weak opening (lead with an instant benefit or intriguing question); a call to action only at the bottom (put one near the top, and use both a button and text links, roughly 6–10 links); stiff words ("buy" not "purchase"); long paragraphs and lines (aim for about 12–16 words per line, single-line paragraphs are fine); not saying "you"; padded subject lines; long sentences (aim for about 10 words on average).
- Subject lines (pp. 143, 147): put the keywords the reader cares about in the first couple of words; keep it short (about 40 characters, since longer gets cut off, though longer can test well for newsletters); signal the benefit at the start; test using the first name.
- Length matches the size of the decision: a cheap impulse item needs a line, a big-ticket booking needs the full argument. Every word must earn its place; cutting about a tenth of filler from a draft is normal (pp. 143–145).
- Structure (pp. 147–150): headline in the preview pane carrying the offer and call to action, personal, urgent, specific, and itself a link; salutation by name or by relevant group ("Dear classic car owner", never "Dear Friend"); first paragraph straight to the point, "Do. Not. Waffle." (p. 148); then why, benefits, credentials, testimonials; for anything over about six lines, repeat the call to action as links; sign off as a named person with a title; legal footer.
- Style: as informal as you dare; avoid strings of adjectives (a short punchy fragment like "Faster." can replace them); send clickers to a landing page that finishes the pitch; images optional and relevant (pp. 150–152).

## Ch 11. Adword (pp. 155–166)
- The action is always a click and you pay for it, so the real measure is conversion on the landing page, not click-through rate (pp. 155–157).
- Two failure diagnoses: no clicks means the ad wording is wrong; clicks with no conversions means the landing page does not match the promise (or the form, privacy or price puts people off) (pp. 158–159).
- Send each ad to a dedicated landing page that delivers exactly what the ad promised, never the home page (p. 159).
- Include the exact, specific keyword phrase in the copy; think of the problem people type, not your solution's name (they search "joint pain", not your product category); say plainly what you sell; state the self-interest benefit ("freedom from debt" beats "debt consolidation") (pp. 160–161).
- The headline is tiny (about 25 characters), so make it the keyword phrase itself; use lines two and three to expand and add the town for local businesses (pp. 160–162).
- Cut needless words, long words, jargon, excess punctuation and superlatives, but don't write in telegram-speak (p. 161).
- For lead generation, sell only the free item, perhaps as a mini story. Run several ad variations to the same landing page and keep the winner. Symbols can save space (a colon before an offer, "+" and "&") (pp. 162–165).

## Ch 12. Article (pp. 167–180)
- Read back issues first and pitch only what fits the outlet and its readers. Readers chose to read to learn something, so corporate puff gets cut (pp. 169–172).
- Respect the word count and house style. Online articles want punchy short sentences and bullets; print can be looser. Dailies need a news hook; monthlies should avoid anything that will date (pp. 172–173).
- Include evidence for the main point (and tell the editor if research disproves the brief), multiple sources, quotes, statistics, and sidebars for lists and mini case studies. Opinion pieces should be forthright, without hedged openings like "it could be argued" (pp. 173–174).
- Leave out hype, showing off, excess technical detail, empty adjectives, redundancies ("orange in colour"), management clichés and unexplained abbreviations (p. 174–176).
- Structure: main point in the first paragraph, like you'd tell a friend at the pub; the rule of three (say what you'll say, say it, recap); headline, then a one- or two-sentence standfirst that summarises and entices ("…gives seven tips"), then an opening that states the problem, a finding or a story (pp. 175–176).
- Bring in a second viewpoint early (a quote or statistic) unless it is opinion; close on a quote, statistic or summary; end with a short author profile and contact line (pp. 176–177).

## Ch 13. Web page (pp. 181–196)
- Know each page's type and purpose. E-commerce pages are catalogue copy: concise features and benefits, and reassuring answers to every "what if?" at each checkout step to cut abandonment. Landing pages follow sales-letter rules (pp. 181–183).
- Failures: offline copy pasted onto the web; insincere "we at Company X" openings; baggy sentences (aim for about 10–12 words on average for consumers, about 16 for business readers, shorter is better); too many in-text links that pull readers off your story; keyword-stuffed copy (pp. 185–187).
- Include, by page type: e-commerce needs features and benefits, price, size, delivery details, testimonials next to the product, an add-to-cart call to action; landing pages for bigger purchases need the full long-letter argument (benefit stories, objections answered, testimonials, captions that sell, multiple calls to action); corporate pages need enough to move the visitor onward (pp. 187–188).
- A page headline should echo the link or menu label that brought the visitor and add meaning ("Our services: online copy that converts more visitors") (p. 188).
- SEO: find the phrases people actually type and weave them in naturally; write for the visitor first, search engines second (p. 188).
- The voice test: does it sound like your best salesperson talking naturally to one customer? (p. 188–189)
- Visitors land on any page, so assume no prior knowledge and give context. Start each page fast: the point in a few words, then expansion, bullets, links and short paragraphs, with the who/what/why up top so people who leave early still take the main message (pp. 190–191).
- Style: direct-response voice, short sentences and words, "you/your" over "I/we", active voice, Anglo-Saxon words, no foreign phrases; open with a conversational sentence and never "Welcome to our website"; warm and a notch less formal than print, without slang; read it aloud (pp. 192–193).
- Images: real people over models; use video or audio where it helps; test each image by asking whether removing it would weaken the page (p. 193).

## Ch 14. Endpiece (pp. 197–198)
- Formats overlap: postcards are short letters or long search ads, posters are giant headlines, and what works in a sales letter works in a sales email or landing page because it's still one person appealing to another (p. 197).
- Build a swipe file of real marketing pieces, and notice anything you see repeated often, since that usually means it is working (p. 197).
- Fastest way to write: plan first. Set your goal, learn everything about the reader, then connect their needs to your goal. What you say matters more than how cleverly you say it (pp. 197–198).

## How to apply
(Invented examples across different formats and businesses.)
- Headlines: run the emergency-headline formula on a meal-kit service: "This helps my customers because it… ends the 6pm 'what's for dinner' panic" becomes "End the 6pm 'what's for dinner' panic". Check it against benefit, news and curiosity.
- Web pages: one page, one job. A plumber's service page leads with the outcome ("Hot water back on today"), puts the call-out fee and the phone action above the fold, and moves the company history to the about page.
- Product and listing descriptions: lead with the benefit, keep the spec as proof, and cut the first paragraph if the piece reads better without it (it usually does).
- Emails: subject line as a specific mini headline; a short first line about the reader; one call to action; a P.S. restating the main benefit. A consultant's follow-up email after a webinar names the one result the reader asked about.
- Case studies: problem, what was tried, what changed, the number that proves it, and a quote in the client's own words. A web agency's case study opens on the client's lost sales, not on the agency.
- Newsletters: useful information first (a florist's "what's in season this week", an accountant's "three deadlines this month"), a quarter or less promotion, one main link.
- Search ads and app store subtitles: treat them the same way. Use the exact search phrase plus one concrete benefit, no hype adjectives, and test one variable at a time.
- Funnel diagnosis with your own data: many impressions with few clicks points to the title, snippet or image; clicks without sales points to the page, the offer or a mismatch between promise and page.
