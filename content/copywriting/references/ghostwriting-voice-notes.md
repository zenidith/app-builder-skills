# Notes: The Art & Business of Ghostwriting (Nicolas Cole, 2023)

Paraphrased technique notes for the brand-voice / founder-voice section of the copywriting skill. These are summaries in our own words, not the book's text. Page numbers are the book's printed page numbers. Voice chapters (6–12) are covered in depth; career, pricing and sales chapters are compressed.

## Introduction (pp. 1–8)
- Experts have more to say than time to write it; the ghostwriter's value is turning their stories, views and insights into finished pieces without costing them hours.
- Calling the service "ghostwriting" (expertise and attention) rather than "freelance writing" (a commodity) is what let the author charge several times more for the same 800-word article.
- Writing in other people's voices for years was, by the author's account, the biggest accelerator of his own craft.

## Ch 1. Ghostwriting Myths (pp. 11–15)
- A ghostwritten piece is "authentic" when the named author confirms it says what they actually think and feel; who typed it is secondary.
- The writer works like a sculptor: the raw material is the client's ideas, and the job is to chip away until the shape is clear.
- The writer is paid to question, stress-test and sharpen the client's thinking, not to take dictation.
- Ideas, stories and insights stay the client's; the writer lends the craft, not their own opinions.

## Ch 2. Freelance Writing vs Ghostwriting (pp. 17–26)
- Readers want to hear from a person, not a brand: posts under an individual's name travel further than company-account posts.
- The core job is two steps: clarify what the person thinks, then amplify it in the right format.
- Three working relationships: (1) repurposing, which turns the client's existing talks, podcasts and posts into new formats; (2) conversational, a recurring call where the writer brings a topic or prompt, lets the client talk, records it and shapes the result; (3) long-form, such as a book.
- Specialise in one service for one type of client; a narrow offer is easier to sell and to deliver well.

## Ch 3. The 7 Benefits of Ghostwriting (pp. 27–36)
- The client is a demanding audience with firm views on how they want to sound, so their feedback is fast, high-quality practice.
- Voice is built, not "found" (p. 34): it is a mix of identifiable attributes such as word choice, cadence and rhythm, structure and format, and whether opinions are backed by data. Short terse sentences read as one writer, long winding ones as another.
- To build or copy a voice, choose and blend those attributes the way a painter mixes colours. It is easier with someone else's voice because you are less attached to it.
- Listen for the "I've never told anyone this" moments in interviews; they become the strongest material.

## Ch 4. The Big Ghostwriting Mistakes (pp. 37–48)
- The best ghostwriters write for themselves too. Practise any format on your own account before selling it.
- Mistake: being "anything for anyone", which makes you a commodity with no switching cost.
- Mistake: treating every client as a blank page. Clients in a niche share problems, so a proven skeleton (for example a thread structure that worked) can be reused with new content.
- Mistake: refusing all free work. An early free sample, offered to sceptics who doubt anyone can capture their voice, is a marketing cost and builds skill.

## Ch 5. How To Niche Down (pp. 49–69)
- People pay for the right words, which means knowing the niche's language and knowing the specific format.
- Positioning formula: one specific problem, for one specific person, solved in one specific way (p. 51). An alternative is to solve an obvious problem in a non-obvious way: list the usual approaches, then ask what else is possible.
- Specificity statement: "I help [person] with [problem] by [way]." It can also work as a call to action.
- Build a personal category first and a personal brand second: promote the topic, not yourself.
- Decide who the content is not for. Writing for everyone reaches no one, and repelling the wrong reader is fine.
- Idea generator (p. 61): run a niche through a long list of content angles (tips, steps, mistakes, lessons, stories, tools, habits, data points, questions, templates, and others) to produce endless topics.
- Flywheel (p. 65): niche → niche content → free opt-in → newsletter → paid offer, with one through-line. Simplicity creates speed; avoid "both".

## Ch 6. Thinking About Thinking About Ghostwriting (pp. 73–76)
- Clients pay for pattern recognition, meaning faster upside and avoided mistakes, not for hours.
- Earn the client's trust by having already done it for yourself.

## Ch 7. How To Ghostwrite Anything, Part I: 5 Steps (pp. 77–89)
- Writing for someone else differs from writing for yourself in one way only: the voice. Most people's voice comes down to one or two identifiable traits (a singer is recognised by one quality of tone). Most clients believe their voice is unique but cannot say what makes it so.
- Step 1, audit existing material (p. 78): collect everything they have already said, including podcasts, talks, newsletters, posts, press quotes and guest appearances. Past content counts as language they have already approved, so it can be reused and reworded freely.
- Step 2, prep the page (p. 79): before writing, set up the scaffolding of the ideal format, including hook style, sentence count and length, bullets, capitals, punctuation, emoji and numbering. Borrow the structure of strong examples, not their content. Keep a swipe file of pieces that worked on you (p. 81).
- Step 3, settle the headline first (p. 82): separate content (what is said) from voice (how it sounds). Get the content right first, because clients care about that most. The headline is the compass and the promise, and everything below it must deliver on that promise. A "mistakes" headline needs one section per mistake.
- Step 4, fill in the headers (pp. 84–86): agree the working headline with the client before the call ends. About 90% of the work is removing, not adding. Let the client talk widely, then pull them back. Sort what they said into piles (mistakes, lessons, tips, later ideas); the biggest pile becomes the piece and the others become future pieces. Check for completeness by asking "any others?".
- Step 5, write in a declarative voice (pp. 87–89): direct, plain, low on jargon, neither harsh nor flowery, polished but not wordy. Clients want to sound confident, not like they are trying too hard. Do not imitate quirks at this stage; write clearly.

## Ch 8. How To Ghostwrite Anything, Part II: The 5 Archetypes of Client Voices (pp. 91–112)
- Voice is the last layer. Plain declarative writing satisfies about 80% of clients; for the rest, make a few small, targeted changes instead of a rewrite.
- Storyteller (pp. 92–95): spot them by openers like "I remember…" or "a few years ago…". A story only earns space when it illustrates a point. Each section is half story, half lesson.
- The 1/3/1 rhythm (p. 94): one strong declarative sentence, three sentences that expand it, then one declarative sentence to close. Use one 1/3/1 block to tell the story compactly and a second for the takeaway.
- Opinionator (pp. 95–97): conviction, absolutes, "most people don't realise…". The lever is emphasis adverbs (really, honestly, obviously, unfortunately). Adding a few to a neutral paragraph makes it sound opinionated.
- Fact Presenter (pp. 97–103): take the adverbs out and put data in, with at least one source per section. Sub-types: mainstream-press stats, peer-reviewed-only, and data that tells a story. Introducing a source ("According to [source]…") carries the voice. Link only that phrase, avoid back-to-back stats, and remember one strong number beats many. Reusing a familiar set of studies saves time.
- Frameworker (pp. 103–107): readers want ideas that are organised and proven. Three moves: turn the advice into a framework, give it a name, and state how many steps it has. Listen for "first we… then… finally…". Name the framework after the outcome it delivers, not a clever pun. Use one named framework per piece and never nest them.
- Profane voice (pp. 107–111): stay close to how the client really talks. Show them their own words and let them decide how much to tone it down. Uncensored, use about one crude word per section. Toned down, keep the attitude but swap in milder words ("just", "seriously", "I have a hard time believing…").
- Combined first draft (pp. 111–112): one story, one strong opinion, one relevant study and one named framework in the first draft gets close to approval. Then adjust the mix.

## Ch 9. The 3 Phases of Nailing a Client's Natural Speaking Voice (pp. 113–123)
- Phase I, transcription (pp. 113–117): record every conversation and write from the transcript. Most of the language is then already the client's own, and their best phrasings are preserved. Tell clients you record; put the phone face down so they forget it; send them the recording and transcript. Ask before using anything sensitive (named clients, testimonials).
- Phase II, edit down (pp. 117–119): (1) cut the intro and outro small talk; (2) cut tangents; (3) cut runs of filler words; (4) skim for the dominant theme and voice signals (stories, opinions, steps); (5) set up the page with a working title and 3–5 subheads; (6) paste transcript chunks under each subhead, then trim and reshape them to the format's best practices.
- Phase III, read it aloud (pp. 119–123). Ask two questions of every line. Can it be said in fewer words? (Cut little words; for example, "It's for this reason that…" becomes "This is why…".) Would this person say this word, phrase or story? (A data-led voice does not swear; an opinionated voice does not hedge with "maybe".) Keep the voice consistent from first word to last.
- "Correctly incorrect" voices (p. 121): some people's casual grammar, slang and broken-off thoughts are their identity. Do not correct them into standard prose.

## Ch 10. Mastering "Close Listening" (pp. 125–140)
- Close listening means attending to what is said, how it is said and what is left unsaid. It makes the speaker feel safe enough to share unpolished thoughts. Talk little and ask a lot.
- The writer directs and the client performs: always arrive with a topic and a working headline.
- Dependable topic types: tips, stats (bring one and ask for their view), steps, habits, lessons, mistakes, examples, questions they ask themselves, resources, and trends.
- The interview aims to collect four ingredients: a personal story, a strong opinion, a relevant data point or trend, and a named framework.
- Story prompt (p. 132): "What was it like the first time you…?" Follow up with what they would do differently, whether they knew what they were doing, how it felt, and why they hesitated.
- Opinion prompt (p. 133): "What's your take on [trend or conventional wisdom]?" Push for where they go against their industry. Follow up with counterpoints, who agrees, and why others believe the opposite.
- Data prompt (p. 134): bring a statistic and ask "Where do you see this going?" Follow up with ceilings, risks, and why people get stuck.
- Framework prompt (p. 136): "If you walked a beginner through this, what are the steps?" Follow up with step one, the next step, any step in between, and the last step. Experts are practitioners, so be patient while they put it into words.
- Specific questions get answers and vague questions get rambling. Ask open questions to draw ideas out, then leading questions to pin them down once you find something good ("It sounds like there's a repeatable process there?").

## Ch 11. Managing Feedback: 5 Steps (pp. 141–154)
- Stance: give your recommendation, then defer: "it's your call". An open revision policy (as many rounds as it takes) shortens how long it takes to learn a voice.
- Step 1: do not react emotionally or reply instantly.
- Step 2: isolate the feedback. "Doesn't sound like me": go through it paragraph by paragraph together and have them point to the lines. "Wrong angle": the problem is the headline, so re-confirm the purpose and leave with a newly approved headline. "Clean it up": decide whether they mean structure (order, sections) or content (examples), and ask section by section. Change nothing until the issue is pinned down.
- Step 3: about 90% of major edits come from a mismatch with the archetype (too much of the wrong ingredient, such as stories for someone who wanted data).
- Step 4: change as few variables as possible. Example: a client who spoke like an opinionator wanted to read like a fact presenter. Adding one sourced stat per section fixed the draft. How someone talks and how they want to appear in writing can differ.
- Step 5: leave margin comments explaining why each choice was made. This shows your thinking, heads off objections, gives senior people a way to take part, and replaces a "grand reveal" with a conversation.
- Endless swapping of adjectives and adverbs signals insecurity or perfectionism. Reassure the client first; if it continues, part ways politely.

## Ch 12. Intellectual Property (pp. 155–158)
- Never make clients sound smarter than they are. Like a jigsaw, only use pieces from that client's box.
- When their material runs thin, curate rather than invent: cite published work with credit.
- Never move one client's distinctive phrasing or named framework to another, even renamed. Curate, or help the second client build their own.

## Part 3 (Chs 13–19, pp. 161–250): business, compressed
- Ch 13, the fear of pricing (pp. 161–168): price talk is a learnable skill. Keep some capacity for pitching higher-paying work. Raise prices yearly with notice.
- Ch 14, three growth levers (pp. 169–177): more clients, higher prices, less time per piece (spend about 10% of time on templates and systems). Sell outcomes, not things: "thought leadership" rather than "blog posts". Five outcome families people buy are money, status, safety, connection and self-esteem; lead with the one this buyer wants most (pp. 175–176).
- Ch 15, five pricing levels (pp. 179–193): per word, per hour, per month, per asset and per outcome. The same deliverable framed by its outcome supports a far higher price. "Whoever frames the problem owns the solution" (p. 181).
- Ch 16, the irresistible offer (pp. 195–210): discounts, hype and stacked bonuses do not make an offer irresistible. Three components: (1) a new, named category that differs from competitors on problem, person or way; avoid "better / faster / cheaper" comparisons and name it in 1–3 words, ideally from the customer's own wording (p. 201). (2) Two chains of language: problem → why it happens → consequence → worst outcome; solution → why it works → benefit → best outcome (p. 204). Describing the vicious cycle in specific terms makes the reader feel seen (p. 206). Build the pitch line in versions, adding one link each time. (3) A clear exchange: big value for small cost, an urgent problem solved for little effort, or a desired outcome delivered fast.
- Ch 17, pricing questions (pp. 211–219): What is it worth to the buyer (pain, cost of leaving it unsolved, upside, their time)? Over what time horizon? What is it worth to you? Offer three tiers with the middle one anchored. List 5–10 problems the buyer has; each needs its own framing.
- Ch 18, getting clients (pp. 221–243): referrals, inbound from your own niche content, and value-first cold outreach. Outreach copy: instead of "I do X, want to hire me?", write "I see you're trying to do X; I think I know why it isn't working yet; can I help?". Lead with a free piece of value (a rewrite of their recent post, a short screen-recorded critique). Write like a peer. Run calls as free consulting. Follow up weekly until a clear no. Take payment before delivery and discount only in exchange for something (testimonial, referral).
- Ch 19, career paths (pp. 245–250): scale across many clients with one asset, or across many assets for one client. The second builds fluency in that client's language, so website, newsletter and social copy become variations of one voice.

## How to apply
(Invented illustrations; swap in the real brand's facts and voice from its project brief.)
- Voice card per brand: a specialty tea shop might be "warm, unhurried, sensory, short sentences, no slang"; a developer tool "dry, exact, numbers first, a little wry, never salesy". Check every draft line against the card before handing it over.
- Founder posts from a recorded call: a 20-minute call with a bakery owner ("the first time you...", "your take on...", "walk a beginner through it") yields three posts in her own phrasing; edit the transcript rather than writing from scratch.
- Problem chain then solution chain on a landing page for a budgeting app: problem (money vanishes by the 20th), why (small subscriptions), consequence (stress every month), worst case (missed rent); then the fix, why it works, the benefit, the best case.
- Outreach to partners or publishers: open with their goal and a free piece of value (a fixed figure, a useful tool, a suggested paragraph), say who you are in one clause, ask one small question.
- Reviews and support messages are already-approved language: lift recurring customer phrases ("it finally just works", "felt like a friend helping") into headlines and bullets, never inventing them.
- Angle list for social and app store text: mistakes beginners make, lessons from our first 100 customers, steps to do X, a contrarian take. Run each brand's niche through the list to fill a content calendar.
- "This doesn't sound like me": go line by line with the owner and change only what they point to.
