# Notes: $100M Offers (Alex Hormozi, 2021)

Paraphrased technique notes for offer design, written in my own words; not the book's text. Page numbers are PDF page indices of the source file (the book's own folios are not reliable in this copy).

## Ch 1. How We Got Here (pp. 17–25)
- Origin story: the author's gym business was near-broke until he changed what he sold and how he packaged it, not the service itself. The lesson he draws: the offer, more than effort or skill, decides whether a business makes money.
- Sets up the book's thesis: a better-structured offer can bring in cash up front and fund growth, instead of chasing customers with discounts.

## Ch 2. Grand Slam Offers (pp. 26–35)
- Core idea, credited to a mentor: make an offer so good that turning it down would feel foolish ("feel stupid saying no", p. 27).
- Definition: an offer is three things together: what you provide, how you take payment, and the terms of the deal (p. 29). It is the first thing a new customer meets in your business.
- Ladder of outcomes: no offer, bad, decent, good, and Grand Slam. Only the last produces real profit and freedom.
- The two pains it fixes: too few customers, and too little cash; the usual symptom is being forced into a price race to the bottom.
- Roadmap: pricing (Section II), building value (III), enhancing the offer (IV), execution (V).

## Ch 3. Pricing: The Commodity Problem (pp. 39–49)
- A business only grows in two ways: get more customers, or make each customer worth more (higher price per purchase or more purchases). Revenue caps at new customers per month times lifetime value (p. 40).
- A commodity is anything sold in many places in near-identical form; its price drifts to the market's lowest efficient level. Differentiated offers are bought on value, not price.
- Grand Slam Offer = an attractive promotion + a value proposition nobody can match + a premium price + an unbeatable guarantee + a payment structure that lets you get paid to acquire the customer (pp. 42–43).
- The goal is to be a "category of one" (p. 42): the buyer cannot compare you line-by-line with anyone, so the choice becomes you or nothing ("sell in a vacuum", p. 43).
- Payoff: more people respond, more of them buy, and they buy at higher prices; fulfillment stays the same.
- Worked idea (pp. 46–48): an agency moves from a standard monthly retainer to an offer that covers the client's ad costs, charges only for results that show up, guarantees a specific number of customers in the first month (or the next month free), and adds bonus playbooks. Result in the example: roughly 2.5x response, 2.5x close rate, a much higher price, and many times more cash collected up front.

## Ch 4. Pricing: Finding the Right Market, a Starving Crowd (pp. 50–62)
- Priority order: market first, offer second, persuasion third. A hungry market rescues a weak pitch; a dying market defeats a great one.
- You channel existing demand; you do not create it.
- Four tests for a market: (1) acute pain, since "the pain is the pitch" (p. 54) and buyers buy when they feel understood; (2) money to pay; (3) easy to reach (groups, lists, communities, shared channels); (4) growing, not shrinking.
- Start inside one of the three evergreen markets (health, wealth, relationships) and then pick a narrower group within it.
- Commit to one niche rather than hopping; most offers fail from impatience, not from the niche.
- Niche pricing ladder (p. 60): the same material sells for many times more as the audience gets more specific (a generic course vs one for a narrow profession in a narrow industry).
- Positioning sentence to aim for: I solve this problem, for this specific person, in this distinctive way.
- If an offer fails, change the offer; do not conclude that you are the problem.

## Ch 5. Pricing: Charge What It's Worth (pp. 63–76)
- People buy to get a deal: perceived value must clearly exceed price. The fix for a weak sale is more value, not a lower price.
- Price can only fall to zero; competing on price only works with a huge cost advantage. There is no prize for being second cheapest, but there is for being the most expensive.
- Raise value first, then raise price, so the buyer still feels they are getting value at a discount (p. 68).
- The typical pricing habit (look at competitors, sit a little under the average, add a little extra) locks you into market-level margins.
- Virtuous cycle of price (pp. 70–72): higher prices make buyers more committed, raise perceived value, attract better clients, produce better results and leave margin to reinvest. Low prices do the opposite.
- Price itself signals value (a wine-tasting study shows the same wine rated better when priced higher, p. 72). Pricing far above the market makes buyers assume something different is on offer.
- A price that stings slightly increases commitment and therefore outcomes.
- Conviction comes from delivery data; the author priced several times above the top competitor and backed it with measured client results (p. 75).

## Ch 6. Value Offer: The Value Equation (pp. 80–95)
- Value = (Dream Outcome x Perceived Likelihood of Achievement) / (Perceived Time Delay x Perceived Effort and Sacrifice) (p. 82).
- The four buyer questions behind it: what do I get, how sure is it, how long until I get it, and what will it cost me in effort.
- Push the top up, push the bottom toward zero. Claims on the top are easy to copy; the strongest businesses win on speed and ease (the bottom).
- Perception beats reality: a transit map showing train arrivals cut perceived waiting more than faster trains would have (p. 84); mirrors near elevators make waits feel shorter (p. 85). Look for cheap psychological fixes before expensive logical ones.
- Dream outcome: reflect the buyer's own desire back to them so they feel understood. The most valued outcomes raise status, so frame benefits by how others will see the buyer afterwards (p. 88).
- Likelihood: people pay a premium for certainty; raise it with proof, specifics, clear inclusions and exclusions, and guarantees.
- Time delay: separate the long-term result from the short-term experience; engineer an early, quick win so the buyer feels progress fast. Speed can beat a free alternative ("Fast beats free", p. 91).
- Effort and sacrifice: done-for-you commands more than do-it-yourself because it cuts effort and raises the odds of success; count both physical and emotional costs.

## Ch 7. Free Goodwill (pp. 96–98)
- Short interlude asking readers for a review; no offer technique beyond the idea that giving value first builds goodwill.

## Ch 8. Value Offer: The Thought Process (pp. 99–103)
- Offer design is divergent thinking: many right answers, some far better than others, unlike a single-answer math problem.
- Exercise: list as many uses for an everyday object as you can in two minutes, varying size, material and shape (p. 100). Apply the same habit to the parts of an offer.
- Start from what the buyer wants, then generate many ways to deliver value, before choosing.

## Ch 9. Creating Your Grand Slam Offer Part I: Problems and Solutions (pp. 104–114)
- Step 1, define the dream outcome in the buyer's terms: sell the destination, not the transport to it (p. 106).
- Step 2, list every problem the buyer will hit: what happens right before and right after they use you, what they will need next, in the order they experience it, in exhaustive detail (p. 107).
- Turn each problem into four objections that map to the value equation: not worth it (outcome), won't work for me (likelihood, usually the most specific), too hard (effort), takes too long or too inconvenient (time). This yields dozens of distinct problems.
- Step 3, rewrite each problem as a solution, usually as a "how to" line (e.g. "confusing and hard" becomes "how to do it simply"). Plain wording beats clever wording.
- One unanswered objection can be enough to block the sale, so cover every perceived problem.

## Ch 10. Creating Your Grand Slam Offer Part II: Trim and Stack (pp. 115–130)
- First version: overdeliver heavily so it sells; later, find cheaper ways to deliver the same promised value. "Create flow. Monetize flow. Then add friction." (p. 117)
- Sales vs fulfillment trade-off: doing more makes selling easier and delivery harder; find the point that sells well and is still easy to deliver.
- Step 4, list every delivery vehicle for each solution (p. 119 onward). Prompts (pp. 122–123): one-to-one, small group or one-to-many; do-it-yourself, done-with-you or done-for-you; live channel (in person, phone, video, chat, text); recorded format (audio, video, written); response speed and hours of availability; and the "10x / one-tenth" test (what would I offer if they paid ten times more, and what if they paid a tenth).
- Don't get attached to one method of delivery; cover every objection with some vehicle.
- Step 5, trim and stack: drop high-cost/low-value items first, then low-cost/low-value; keep low-cost/high-value and the few high-cost/high-value items. Judge "value" with the four drivers.
- Favor assets built once and reused for every customer (templates, guides, tools); keep personal time for the biggest value adds.
- Final format: each item reads problem, then solution, then a benefit-led bundle name, then how it is delivered, each with its own stated value (p. 129). The bundle should total far more than the price (the example stacks several thousand dollars of stated value against a few hundred dollars of price).
- The finished stack does three things: answers every perceived problem, gives the seller conviction, and cannot be compared like-for-like.

## Ch 11. Enhancing the Offer: Scarcity, Urgency, Bonuses, Guarantees and Naming (pp. 134–144)
- Overview of five enhancers: scarcity, urgency, bonuses, guarantees, names. They work on supply and demand perception without changing the core.
- When demand rises, cut supply rather than adding more (p. 137); people want what is limited, what others want, and what few have.
- Delay satisfying demand to grow it: announce quietly, then tease benefits, then loudly launch (p. 140).
- Demand follows an 80/20 pattern: a small slice will pay several times more. Selling fewer units at a higher price can earn as much and leave pent-up demand for the next round.
- "The longer you delay the ask, the bigger the ask you can make" (p. 143). Keep supply slightly below demand.

## Ch 12. Enhancing the Offer: Scarcity (pp. 145–154)
- Scarcity implies demand; the side that needs the deal less holds the power. Fear of missing out outweighs desire for gain.
- Three types: limited quantity (seats or slots, total or per period), limited bonuses, and items never offered again.
- Products: limited runs and colors; sell out and say so publicly; release on a steady cadence.
- Services: cap total clients (with a waitlist), cap new clients per week and state how many spots remain, or cap each cohort (doors open, doors close) (p. 152).
- Honest scarcity is the easiest: decide your real capacity for the period and publish it, e.g. that you are three-quarters full this week (p. 153). It doubles as social proof.
- Extreme version: a tiny number of high-priced, direct-access slots (p. 154).

## Ch 13. Enhancing the Offer: Urgency (pp. 155–161)
- Scarcity limits quantity; urgency limits time. Deadlines must be real.
- Rolling cohorts: a new group starts on a set day, so there is always a near start date to beat (p. 157). The final hours of a weekly window produce a large share of sales (p. 158). For late buyers, offer a speed-up bonus or frame the wait as an advantage.
- Rolling seasonal urgency: give the same promotion a new seasonal or monthly name with real start and end dates and a countdown; the author's top tactic for local businesses.
- Pricing or bonus urgency: change the price or the bonus every few weeks (p. 159), so the deadline is on the deal, not the service. Announced price rises clear a pipeline of fence-sitters.
- Exploding opportunity: an advantage that shrinks over time (e.g. an arbitrage window) (p. 160).

## Ch 14. Enhancing the Offer: Bonuses (pp. 162–171)
- The same offer broken into separate parts and stacked sells better than one lump; anchor the core price, then add item after item.
- Widen the gap between price and value with bonuses instead of discounting the core.
- In a one-to-one sale: ask for the sale first; if yes, reveal bonuses as a delight; if no, add a bonus that kills their stated obstacle and ask again whether it is fair (p. 165).
- Bonus checklist (pp. 165–166): always include them; give each a benefit-led name; say how it relates to their problem, what it is, how you made or found it, and how it makes things faster or easier; add proof; paint the after-state; give each a stated price and justify it.
- Tools, checklists, templates and scripts beat more training; each bonus should remove one specific objection or solve the buyer's next problem.
- The stated value of the bonuses should exceed the core offer's.
- Add scarcity or urgency to bonuses (only with this purchase, only for the next few buyers, only today).
- Use partners' products as bonuses: a complementary non-competing business provides a free service for exposure, and can pay you a referral fee (p. 167).

## Ch 15. Enhancing the Offer: Guarantees (pp. 172–189)
- The biggest objection is risk; a strong guarantee can lift conversion several times over (p. 173). State it boldly and with a reason why (p. 174).
- Run the numbers: a stronger guarantee usually adds more sales than it adds refunds (worked example: more buyers at a higher refund rate still nets more, p. 174).
- Formula (p. 175): if you don't get result X within time Y, we will do Z. A guarantee needs a concrete consequence, not just a promise.
- Four types: unconditional (no questions, effectively a trial); conditional (tied to the actions that produce success; can be better than money back); anti-guarantee (all sales final, with a strong reason why); implied (performance pricing, revenue share, pay only on results, often with a minimum fee, p. 187).
- Stack guarantees (e.g. a short unconditional window plus a longer conditional one) (p. 177).
- Name the guarantee vividly rather than calling it a "satisfaction guarantee" (p. 179).
- Broad guarantees suit low-priced consumer offers; specific, conditional ones suit high-priced or business offers.
- Conditional variants (pp. 180–184): multiple-of-price refund, keep serving free until the result is reached (the author's favorite), extra service period, refund as credit, personal help until the result, covering travel or costs, release from contract, delaying the next payment until the first result is achieved.
- To create your own: ask what the buyer fears most and reverse that fear; consider time, emotion and outside costs. Specific and creative beats generic. A guarantee amplifies a good product and cannot save a bad one.

## Ch 16. Enhancing the Offer: Naming (pp. 190–201)
- People are drawn to things that seem made for them; an outcome-led name pulls far more than a plain description of the service (p. 191).
- Offers tire fast, especially locally. Refresh the wrapper, not the offer.
- Naming formula, "MAGIC" (pp. 193–195): a magnetic reason why (free, discount, launch, season, anniversary); announce the avatar (as local and specific as possible); give the goal (the specific, tangible dream outcome); indicate a time interval (check ad-platform rules on quantified claims); complete with a container word (challenge, blueprint, getaway, intensive, sprint and similar).
- Use three to five of the parts, in any order; keep it short; rhyme or alliteration help only if natural (p. 195).
- Test two or three names, keep the winner as the control, keep testing. Name every bonus and sub-item with the same formula (p. 198).
- When results fade, change in this order (pp. 198–199): ad images, body copy, headline/wrapper, the seasonal theme, the duration, the free or discount element, and only last the whole pricing and offer structure.
- Local note (p. 200): local trust makes selling easier, but a small radius wears out offers quickly, so vary the presentation often while keeping the value stack.

## Section V. Your First $100,000 (pp. 205–209)
- Personal story plus a recap: don't be a commodity, choose a hungry market, charge a lot, build value with the four drivers, create the offer in five steps, stack and trim, then add scarcity, urgency, bonuses, guarantees and a name. Points to the follow-up book on lead generation.

## How to apply
All sketches below are invented illustrations for drafting; any real product's prices, inclusions and guarantees need the owner's confirmation before use.

- Value equation pass on a SaaS scheduling tool. Dream outcome: a full calendar with no back-and-forth emails. Likelihood: customer counts, named case studies, a free trial. Time delay: set up in five minutes, first booking the same day. Effort: connects to the calendar they already use; nothing to learn. Copy should hit all four, especially the bottom two.
- Problem list for an online photography course: "What if I only have a phone?", "I've started courses and never finished", "Will I understand the settings?", "What if I don't like it?". Each becomes a "how to" line or a feature bullet: lessons shot on a phone, 10-minute lessons, a plain-words glossary, a refund window.
- Named stack for a local dentist's new-patient offer: "First Visit Check & Clean" as the core; "No-Surprise Treatment Plan" (written prices before any work); "Evening Appointment Priority". Low-cost, reusable assets add value without adding cost.
- Guarantee built on the biggest fear: a home-cleaning service promises "If we miss a spot, we come back within 24 hours and re-clean it free", with a vivid name rather than "satisfaction guaranteed". Only offer what the business can honor.
- Honest scarcity and urgency: a coaching programme with 12 places per cohort states the real number left; a bakery's seasonal box runs only for the weeks the fruit is in season; seasonal names give rotating, dated wrappers without changing the core product.
- MAGIC naming: "The 30-Day Desk Posture Reset for Remote Workers" covers the avatar, goal, time frame and container. Test two or three variants and keep the winner as the control.
- Low-cost, high-value assets as lead-ins or bonuses: a pricing calculator, a checklist, a template, a shortlist. They solve one problem completely and cost almost nothing to give again.
- Copy order for any landing page or listing: pain mirrored first, then the dream outcome, then the stack with stated values, then proof and guarantee, then the price and one call to action.
