# Notes: The Art and Business of Online Writing (Nicolas Cole, 2020)

Paraphrased technique notes for the copywriting skill, not the book's text. Page refs are PDF page indexes of the source file (pp. 1–341).

## Introduction: Logging In (pp. 9–38)
- Online writing is a game with levels. You improve by repeating the basics daily (e.g. one Q&A answer a day for a year).
- Relatability beats credentials: say exactly when and how you learned the lesson. Universal life lessons from personal experience spread; niche topics stay modest.
- Information plus personal stakes or story gets featured; information alone rarely does. Open on a specific, vivid claim at the height of the story, not a definition.
- When a piece takes off, add a relevant offer or link to it at once, and keep publishing so the spike becomes growth.

## Ch 1. Want to Start Writing Online? Don't Start a Blog (pp. 39–48)
- A blog is really a media or store business; a website is a business card (who you are, what you do, proof); online writing means publishing where an audience already exists (reviews, LinkedIn, guest posts).
- A new blog starts at zero traffic. Write where readers already are first, and judge your website by its credibility signals.
- A company blog makes sense when aimed at search keywords like an industry publication, or as a niche encyclopedia of answers tied to a product and email opt-ins.
- Test topics in public, then expand the one that resonates. Readers check third-party platforms for social proof.

## Ch 2. The New Way to Think About Being a Writer (pp. 49–58)
- Data is the best guide. Build the audience before the product, and use post numbers to choose the title, structure and concept of bigger assets.
- Data flywheel (p. 54): do more of what got views, retell stories that got comments, and copy your own best headline and format patterns.
- Signals: likes mean approval; shares mean "others need this" or "this represents me"; comments mean it provoked a reaction; views mean it struck a chord.
- "Practice in public" (p. 55): publish, watch the reaction, adjust.

## Ch 3. How the Online Writing Game Works: 7 Levels (pp. 59–92)
- Level 1: play on purpose and study the competition. Level 2: pick a category; readers choose the shelf first, then the best writer on it. Learn its language, formats and leading voices.
- Level 3, style: place yourself between educating and entertaining. Distinctive means sitting somewhere unexpected for your category, since most industries sound alike. Find it from data.
- Level 4, speed (p. 68): readers decide within seconds, so lead with the main point and cut backstory. "Rate of Revelation": each sentence must add something new. If deleting everything after a paragraph's first sentence loses nothing, the paragraph is slow.
- Level 5, specificity (pp. 71–76): copy for everyone lands with no one. Specific copy makes the right reader say yes. Narrow step by step and ask of each sentence whether it could be more specific.
- Level 6, credibility (pp. 77–85). Implied: quality, photo, a clear bio (not cute), professional images, clean formatting. Perceived: press logos, testimonials, badges, follower counts (it backfires if the content doesn't deliver). Earned: recency, frequency, volume, view counts, one standout piece. Show every badge in bios and intros.
- Level 7: different beats better. New categories form at unexpected crossings (audience x genre, genre x genre, a familiar genre in an unusual tone). Then explain how you differ from your neighbours (your point of view).

## Ch 4. Where You Should Be Writing Online (pp. 93–121)
- Choose platforms by their traits: a large user base, relevance to your category, and analytics.
- Big publications give logo credibility, backlinks and social exposure, but no guaranteed views, little room for self-promotion and no audience capture. Pieces about the reader beat pieces about the writer.
- Match platform to form (Q&A sites for advice, Medium for opinion, LinkedIn for business). Join a platform once it is established but before ads throttle organic reach; move on when reach drops.
- Republish your back catalogue on new platforms, crediting the original.

## Ch 5. How Writing on Social Platforms Works (pp. 122–154)
- Spend more hours creating than consuming. Publish consistently for about six months before judging (at least every other week; several a week is best). The algorithm is a roulette wheel: more spins, more wins.
- Rotate three content buckets (industry, role, personal interest) and let data pick winners. Use a proven topic to carry a passion topic.
- Study the top creators and ask why each repeated pattern exists (all-caps titles, one-sentence paragraphs, hero photos). Copy it, then make it yours.
- Growth tactics: collaborate with peers your size, comment on hot news in your niche, engage thoughtfully with 10–15 slightly bigger peers, and mix broad and niche hashtags.
- Publish once per platform, share links often. On social, a repost within 24–48 hours that changes one variable (headline, hashtags) is a valid retest.
- Syndication pitch: who you are, what you write, why it fits their audience, a free republishing offer, your 3 best links.

## Ch 6. How to Always Write Something People Will Want to Read: 5 Forms (pp. 155–171)
- Five forms (p. 155): Actionable Guide, Opinion, Curated List, Story, Credible Talking Head. Name the form before writing, then aim for the best version of it in your category.
- Ask: which form? How could it be the ultimate version (what do competitors' versions miss)? Can it be more specific, or am I cramming? A guide title over list content breaks the promise. Keep one topic per piece and split the extras out.
- Length is not value. A guide should be worth bookmarking. Ways to beat rivals (pp. 158–159): more depth, the opposite voice to the field, better organisation, reframing the problem, insider vs plain language, giving away what others charge for.
- Opinion (pp. 160–161): build on data (even one striking fact), curated quotes, an outside-the-industry insight, a historical story, or one very clear sentence.
- Curated list (pp. 162–165): every item fits the promise and is quick to grasp (what it is plus one line). Use fresh examples. Subheads as full statements so skimming gives the gist. A short intro covers why this list and who is writing it.
- Story (pp. 166–168): start at the peak moment so the reader is already inside it. If it only gets good four paragraphs in, delete the first four. One-sentence paragraphs keep the pace up.
- Credible Talking Head (pp. 169–170): stay where you are most qualified. Put one credibility clause before the advice ("I learned this building ..."). Argue what not to do. Make the design and photos unlike the category's.

## Ch 7. The Perfect Post: How to Write Headlines People Can't Help but Read (pp. 172–192)
- Every format has a headline: the first line of a post or tweet, a title and subtitle, a homepage hero.
- A headline (roughly 5–15 words) must say three things at once (p. 173): what it's about, who it's for, and the promise (the result or the problem solved). The "Curiosity Gap" is the promise without giving away the answer. Answering one of the three is weak, two is good, three is exceptional.
- Clarity is the creative work. A vague headline hides the topic, an unspecific one tells readers it isn't for them, a weak promise gets ignored.
- Numbers aren't clickbait; broken promises are. Avoid lazy hype ("you won't believe"). Use audience + problem + concrete promise instead.
- Two-part title (p. 175): a main title plus a very specific subtitle that adds a second promise and the reason to believe you can deliver.
- Write the headline first (p. 177). It is a micro-version of the piece; if it won't fit in about twelve words, the post won't be clear either.
- Audience size follows the size of the question (p. 178). Universal questions (happiness, money, relationships) reach the masses; niche questions reach niches. The best of both: a niche topic tied to a universal outcome in the promise. Wide audiences need simple, universal words; niche audiences need very specific ones.
- Anatomy (pp. 181–186): an opener (the first few words matter most because readers scan the start and end; a leading number like "The 1" signals conviction and brevity), then the "what" noun (reason, way, mistake; pick the most intriguing one, because "when you confuse, you lose", p. 183), then a sparing connector with strong verbs, then the "who", then the promise at the end: emotional, vivid, specific, ideally measurable.
- Proven formats (pp. 186–188): big numbers; dollar figures; credible names; something that just happened ("just", "today", "now"); a success story; two things that shouldn't go together; calling out the industry or audience; a topic within the topic (a parenthetical second half); question/answer; "X ways" lists. Combine them.
- Rewrite process (pp. 189–191): write many versions (big media sites write dozens per article). (1) Say it in as many words as you need. (2) Make the promise bigger and more emotional. (3) Replace vague words with 2–3 words that hit the reader's interests. (4) Cut small filler words (if, when, does, it, too, for). Start over if it still isn't strong.
- Power modifiers (p. 191) signal importance or difference: "little-known", "small but powerful", "crucial", "eye-opening", "unforgettable".

## Ch 8. How to Structure the Perfect Post (pp. 193–249)
- Make the point in fewer sentences: 5, then 3, then 2, then 1. Basic shape: intro, X main points, conclusion.
- The intro must answer: what is this about, is it for me, what's the promise, and why should I believe you'll deliver. The first sentence matters most: short, clear, the whole point in about ten words or fewer. Never open with a long first paragraph.
- Intro rhythms (pp. 196–209): 1/3/1 (one-line opener; three lines that clarify, add credibility and round out; a one-line close that also hands off to the main points); 1/5/1 for more context (don't go to 1/7/1); 1/3/2/1 and 1/5/2/1; 1/4/1/1 (a double close: the conclusion, then why it matters or the emotional benefit).
- For longer intros, join two 1/3/1 blocks with a subhead (one point per chunk), or go faster with 1/3/subhead/3/1. Variants: 1/3/1 plus bullets (the closing line introduces a list that compresses 5+ sentences), or 1 plus subhead (one framing line, then straight into the first point). Weak bullets backfire.
- Visual weight: a dense block looks heavy before it is read. Vary paragraph lengths like a wave: short, longer, medium, short (p. 211).
- Many readers skip the intro and skim the subheads, then decide. The usual sweet spot is 800–1,200 words (p. 211). Write backwards: draft the skeleton of main points first, then give each its share of space.
- Main-point structures: 1/2/5/3/1 for three or fewer points (p. 213: opener, why it matters, the expansion with story or insight and new information, a recap, the takeaway); 1/3/1 for four or more (opener, clarify, why care, then go straight to a mini-conclusion and skip long explanation); a run of one-line paragraphs for openings, endings and emphasis (p. 218; overused it sounds monotone, so follow it with a longer paragraph); a main point plus 1–3 sentences each for 15–20 points (p. 220).
- More points means less explanation for each. To make a piece longer, add points, not padding. Always ask how it could be shorter and faster.
- Every section opens and closes with a clear, declarative sentence, with the substance in the middle.
- Subheads must be full statements, not one-word labels, so the subheads alone give the gist. In non-list pieces, bold the key insight sentences so skimming the bold alone gives the gist (p. 210, 223); in labelled sections, bold only the opening half-sentence of each paragraph (p. 235).
- Rhythm tools (p. 227–235): short/long/long/short paragraphs, never three long ones in a row; vary sentence length inside paragraphs; put a single declarative line before and after long paragraphs; prose repetition ("If you X, you're doing Y" four times) to add information fast; parallel phrasing across subheads to signal deliberate organisation.
- Conclusions are optional online (pp. 237–245). Options: end on the last point with no sign-off; fold the takeaway into the final main point; a bulleted summary plus one closing line (good for guides); or close with a strong new opinion, a clear should or shouldn't. Never just restate. Avoid generic "check out my site" plugs.
- Language (pp. 246–249): write for an everyday reader (no semicolons; swap words most people wouldn't know for simpler ones); write the way you speak; use long sentences only as a deliberate contrast; alternate sentence and paragraph lengths; write confidently and declaratively, because clear agreement or disagreement beats a shrug.

## Ch 9. How to Talk About Yourself Without Making Your Writing All About "You" (pp. 250–266)
- The more people promote themselves, the less they listen (p. 250). The reader is the main character, not you or the product. Mention yourself only to give context or credibility.
- "Golden Intersection" (p. 251): answer the reader's question and tell an engaging story at once, with yourself in it only as the context for how you learned the point.
- Product posts that make the product the hero do badly. Write about the reader's pain, and bring the product in around the 3rd or 4th main point as the story behind that point. The section's first sentence names the reader's problem.
- "For context" credentials (pp. 252–263): drop an achievement as an aside while delivering value. Self-talk never equals reader-talk (1–2 sentences); keep it relevant, embedded in a bigger point and casually phrased; never give it a spotlight section. Make your business the setting for the insight.
- Calls to action (pp. 264–266): a short sign-off at the end; better, the CTA as a credible example inside a main point with a proof number ("10,000+ people have taken my free course", linked); or a link on a descriptive phrase. Never hard-sell.

## Ch 10. Your Content Roadmap: A "Sticky Web" (pp. 267–289)
- Volume wins. Timeless pieces earn for years; timely ones spike and fade (5,000 views a year for ten years beats 10,000 in a day, p. 269), so keep timely pieces a small share.
- After 3–6 months of exploring, narrow to three buckets: a general audience (universal topics through your lens), a niche audience (your expertise and its specific pains), and your company or industry.
- Endless Idea Generator (p. 274): form x idea type (explanations, habits, mistakes, lessons, tips, stories, timely events) x why me (expert / gathered experts / clearest opinion) gives an outline plus a headline. The roadmap (pp. 276–287) is 3 buckets, then 3+ topics each, then many working headlines per topic.
- Before writing, ask: does it fit a bucket, is it timeless, and have I written it before (if so, what is the new lens)? Retell signature stories through many lenses.

## Ch 11. Pillar Pieces (pp. 290–307)
- Expand proven pieces into bigger assets: combine related pieces, add expert quotes, statistics, a personal story per point, and more examples. Expand with substance, never padding.
- Landing pages greet visitors with exactly what they came for ("looking for X? you're in the right place", p. 292). Send off-platform traffic to the most generous Pillar Piece, not the homepage, because every click costs attention.
- Build a road where each step is a little more valuable: article, deeper resource, pillar, opt-in or product. Link the pillar inside a relevant main point; never present an offer out of context.
- Opt-ins must be more specific than "subscribe for more": a short email course on one problem, case studies, exclusive content, resources by level, templates.
- People buy solutions to urgent problems, not products (p. 304, quoting Craig Clemens). Paid tiers sell on convenience, specificity and depth (p. 305); give most of your best material away free.

## Ch 12. How to Make Money Online as a Writer (pp. 308–336)
- Prerequisites: publish regularly (about 4+ a month), clear buckets, and a site that says plainly who you are.
- Models: ads (needs huge volume and erodes trust), exclusivity (products, courses, paid newsletters; about 1,000 true fans can be enough), and services. Weave affiliate or sponsor mentions into a main point, with disclosure.
- Paid material must beat the free. Value is the result the words drive (action, clarity of message), not the word count; every piece of writing sells an idea.

## Ch 13. The 1 Habit Every Writer Needs (pp. 337–339)
- The habit behind every result in the book: writing a lot, daily, as a discipline.

## How to apply
(Invented examples across different businesses and channels.)
- Headlines via the four-pass rewrite: "Our new feature" → "Send invoices automatically" → "Get paid 9 days faster with automatic invoice reminders" → cut filler. Each pass adds who, what or the promise.
- A niche content site or blog: use an idea generator (mistakes beginners make, little-known options near a well-known one, X vs Y comparisons). Use full-statement subheads so a skimmer gets the article from the subheads alone.
- Two-part titles for guides and courses: "Learn Watercolour in 30 Days: A 15-Minute-a-Day Plan for Total Beginners".
- Outreach emails: subject as a mini headline (who + promise). First sentence names the recipient's benefit. One "for context" clause about you, a specific number, one ask.
- Credibility without bragging: a consultant writes "after 140 audits, the same three mistakes show up" instead of "I am an expert". Talk about yourself through the reader's gain.
- App store and social: the subtitle works as the second half of a two-part title (a specific outcome). Social posts open at the peak moment of a customer story, then backfill.
- Specificity pass on any draft: replace each vague word with a number, a name, a time or a place ("fast" → "in 48 hours").
