# 1. The Adweek Copywriting Handbook, Part A (Section One)

Paraphrased technique notes for internal use, not the book's text. Page refs are PDF page indexes of the source file (pp. 1–135: front matter, Introduction, Section One "Understanding the Process", chapters 1–17, and the opening preview of Section Two).

### Front matter, Introduction and Section One preview (pp. 1–33)

- The author's working definition of the job: copy exists to get a person to trade money they worked hard for in exchange for a product or service (p. 28). Every other rule in the book serves that one outcome.
- Draft the offer first as a print direct-response ad, even if it will finally live on a website, catalog, TV spot or email. That format forces you to find the real core of what you sell; other media are adaptations of that master version (p. 32).
- The principles were unchanged when the book was revised for newer media; only examples and figures changed (p. 32). Treat the techniques as medium-independent.
- Section One teaches the thinking process and the steps to write an ad; Section Two covers what has worked in practice (pp. 32–33, 134).

### Ch 1. General Knowledge (pp. 34–37)

- A copywriter's raw material is the sum of everything they have experienced and know; broad curiosity and wide life experience are working assets, not hobbies (pp. 34–35).
- Ideas are new combinations of old pieces. The more pieces you hold, the more combinations you can make (pp. 35–36).
- Lateral thinking drill: pick three random, unrelated words and force them into a pitch for the product. It shakes you off the obvious feature-list angle (p. 36).
- The dictionary is a creative tool: browsing words sparks unexpected connections between distant concepts (pp. 36–37).
- Being accountable for results (running your own business, owning every word) teaches faster than writing in isolation (p. 37).

### Ch 2. Specific Knowledge (pp. 38–45)

- Become an expert on the product before writing. Keep asking questions of the people who make it until something distinctive surfaces; one engineer's offhand detail became the whole concept of a successful watch ad, "Laser Beam Digital Watch" (pp. 38–39).
- Know the customer as well as the product. Each product has a "nature" in the buyer's mind, and you must find it (pp. 39–40).
- Some products are bought only when the need becomes personal (e.g. after a friend has a problem). For those, plant the idea early and keep it present so you are remembered when the moment comes; readers clip and save ads and buy later (pp. 40–42).
- Don't lead with fear or statistics if they don't move this buyer. For a burglar alarm he skipped crime figures and stressed what buyers actually worried about (reliability, easy self-installation), plus a credible endorser (pp. 41–43).
- A product's apparent weakness can become its selling point when you explain it honestly and frame it from the buyer's side (Pocket CB example) (pp. 44–45).

### Ch 3. Practice, Practice, Practice (pp. 46–49)

- Axiom 1 (p. 47), paraphrased: copywriting is a mental process that draws on all your experience and specific knowledge, which you then turn into words on a page in order to sell.
- There is no single correct method. Start writing and keep writing; volume builds skill (pp. 46–48).
- The first draft is supposed to be rough. Get the ideas and the feeling out without judging; the craft lies in reworking (cutting, adding, reordering) afterwards (pp. 47–48).

### Ch 4. The Purpose of All the Graphic Elements of an Ad (pp. 50–53)

- A print ad has ten elements: headline, subheadline, photo, caption, body copy, paragraph headings, logo, price, response device (order form/phone), and layout (pp. 50–51).
- Students usually assign each element its own job (grab attention, show the product, identify the brand). His point: they all share one job (p. 52).
- Axiom 2 (p. 52), paraphrased: every element in the ad exists mainly to get the first sentence of the copy read.
- The caption is singled out as important and often read (p. 50), so it deserves real effort, not a throwaway label.

### Ch 5. The First Sentence (pp. 54–57)

- Keep the opening sentence very short, even a fragment, with no hard words, so it is effortless to start (e.g. a two- or three-word line) (pp. 54–55).
- The first sentence is the locomotive: it builds momentum for everything behind it (p. 55).
- Axiom 3 (p. 56), paraphrased: the only job of the first sentence is to get the second sentence read; then each sentence must pull the reader into the next.
- Don't open with the sales pitch; first earn the reader's continued attention (pp. 55–56).
- Magazines often set the opening in larger type to make starting easy; copy can use the same trick (p. 55).

### Ch 6. Creating the Perfect Buying Environment (pp. 58–61)

- The opening paragraphs and layout set the mood in which the reader decides whether to buy, the way a store's atmosphere does (a gallery that drew him into its back room) (pp. 58–60).
- Match the environment to the offer: bargain offers want a busy, price-driven, energetic feel; premium offers want restraint, class, confidence and calm (pp. 59–60).
- Unlike a retailer, a writer fully controls the environment through word choice, tone, honesty and design. A consistent house format builds a recognizable, trusted environment (p. 60).
- Axiom 4 (p. 61), paraphrased: the layout and first few paragraphs must create the buying environment most suited to selling this product.

### Ch 7. Resonating with the Reader (pp. 62–67)

- Selling sequence in an ad: set the environment, win attention (headline), hold it (subhead, photo, caption), then make the pitch (body copy) (p. 62).
- Readers buy from people they like and trust and only when the message feels relevant to them. One "that doesn't apply to me" or one doubtful claim can lose them (pp. 62–64).
- Build agreement early with statements that are plainly true for the reader, like a salesperson who earns a string of small yeses before asking for the order (pp. 63–64).
- Tie the product to something the reader is already feeling or reading about in the news; an emergency food product was framed around a current shortage scare instead of as dull "insurance" (pp. 64–65).
- Speak the reader's language and mirror their style. A magazine that wrote its sales piece in the advertiser's own voice won the business (pp. 65–67).
- Axiom 5 (p. 67), paraphrased: get the reader nodding yes and in harmony with accurate, truthful statements as they read.

### Ch 8. The Slippery Slide (pp. 68–77)

- Each element must make the next one irresistible: headline into subhead into first sentence into second, and so on to the end, like a slide you can't stop on (pp. 68–69).
- Once someone has read more than about a quarter of an ad, they are very likely to finish it (p. 70). The hard work is all at the start.
- Axiom 6 (p. 72), paraphrased: readers should feel so compelled that they can't stop until they've read everything.
- An unexpected opening angle creates pull: run down the product, or admit its flaws, then reveal its real merits (the "Magic Baloney" ad); or open with a surprising, even shocking framing (pp. 70–72).
- Open with an odd story or news item and connect it to the product, even loosely, with a promise that the link will be explained shortly. Keep a file of unusual stories for this (pp. 72–75).
- Graphic devices help: larger type for the opening, or starting copy on one page and continuing it on another (pp. 75–77).

### Ch 9. Assumed Constraints (pp. 78–81)

- Many limits are self-imposed habits, like a grown elephant still held by a small stake, or solvers of the nine-dot puzzle who never draw outside the box (pp. 78–79).
- Question the "rules": that busy executives won't read mail, that some products can't sell by mail or at a high price. He sold items others said couldn't be sold that way (pp. 79–81).
- Axiom 7 (p. 81), paraphrased: break out of assumed constraints; relate the problem to unrelated things to find solutions.

### Ch 10. Seeds of Curiosity (pp. 82–87)

- End a paragraph with a short teaser line that makes reading the next paragraph feel necessary, e.g. "But there's more." or "Let me explain." (p. 82). It carries readers through the slower parts.
- Works like a TV teaser before a commercial break. Use sparingly so it doesn't feel mechanical (pp. 82–83).
- Bigger version: promise early that something interesting will be revealed later, and pay it off near the end (pp. 83–84).
- A poor result can come from a weak piece and also from the wrong list. Test small (a few thousand names) before rolling out big (pp. 84–86).
- Once a reader is about halfway through, they are effectively committed (p. 86).
- Axiom 8 (p. 86), paraphrased: keep the copy interesting and the reader engaged through curiosity.

### Ch 11. Copy as Emotion (pp. 88–93)

- Three ideas: every word carries an emotional charge and a little story; a good ad is an emotional outpouring of ideas; people buy on emotion and then justify the purchase with logic (p. 89).
- Give the buyer the logical reasons too (specs, value, comparisons), because they need them to justify the decision, but lead with feeling (pp. 89–91).
- When a technical feature is hard to explain, dramatize the situation where it pays off instead of explaining how it works. Rivals explained the tech and failed; his ad showed a scene from the buyer's life (pp. 90–92).
- Choose words for their feel as well as their meaning; small swaps (e.g. a "cottage" instead of a "motel," "fix" instead of "repair") can change response measurably. Never change the facts to get the feel (p. 93).
- Write the first draft for emotion; edit later for precision (pp. 91–93).

### Ch 12. Selling the Concept, Not the Product (pp. 94–99)

- Axiom 9 (p. 94), paraphrased: don't sell the product itself, sell a concept (its positioning, big idea or unique angle).
- A genuinely new product can be its own concept. As a category matures, you need a differentiating angle (thinnest, built-in feature X, made with technology Y) (pp. 94–96).
- The product name can carry the concept (a "pocket" version of something big) (pp. 95–96).
- Several products can be bundled under one concept. A challenge framing (beat a champion) can beat an endorsement (pp. 96–97).
- Price changes the concept: the same product read as serious at a higher price, as a gadget a bit lower, and as a toy at the lowest, with near-identical copy (p. 97). Price is positioning.
- The drama is usually already inside the product; dig it out rather than invent it (pp. 97–98).

### Ch 13. The Incubation Process (pp. 100–103)

- Preparation phase: soak up everything about the product and customer, jot candidate headlines and points, picture the result you want (pp. 100–101).
- Then stop and do something enjoyable; the subconscious keeps working and the idea tends to surface on its own (pp. 101–102).
- Incubation runs better with some pressure (deadline, ego, expectations), a creative bent and the right surroundings (pp. 102–103).
- Axiom 10 (p. 103), paraphrased: the subconscious uses all your knowledge to solve the problem; time, creative orientation, environment and ego decide how well.
- When the idea arrives, write it freely without worrying about spelling or grammar; discipline belongs to editing. Incubation plus the draft is about half the job; editing is the other half (p. 103).

### Ch 14. How Much Copy Should You Write? (pp. 104–109)

- Copy is never too long if the reader acts; it must be compelling, relevant and about the reader's own interests (p. 104).
- Thought experiment: a newspaper story naming you as heir to a fortune would be read to the last word. Intense personal relevance makes length irrelevant (pp. 104–105).
- Interest is greatest when a category is new or the buyer is actively shopping; then they want every detail (p. 105).
- Write as much as the full sales argument needs, no more and no less (pp. 105–107).
- Longer copy is needed for higher prices, for unfamiliar or unusual products, and for prices so low they look suspicious. Educating the reader raises perceived value (pp. 106–107).
- Short copy can work when the product is familiar, cheap, and the visuals and context (e.g. a catalog) already set the environment (p. 107).
- Axiom 11 (p. 108), paraphrased: write enough copy to get the reader to take the action you ask for.

### Ch 15. The Art of Personal Communication (pp. 110–115)

- Write as one person talking to one person ("I" to "you"), not a company addressing a crowd. Rewriting a corporate invitation into a personal note shows the difference (pp. 110–111).
- Personal, specific details create warmth: a named sender and a concrete detail (e.g. "I showed your order to our president") beat a generic thank-you (pp. 111–112).
- A byline or a named founder lets you write in first person and tell a true story, which builds closeness and trust (pp. 112–114).
- Candor and a small-company, human voice can be powerful, even admitting shortcomings (p. 112).
- Fit the personality of the medium where the copy appears (pp. 114–115).
- Axiom 12 (p. 115), paraphrased: every communication should be personal, from writer to individual, whatever the medium.

### Ch 16. The Copy Sequence (pp. 116–123)

- Copy should anticipate the reader's next question and answer it in the next sentence or paragraph, as a good salesperson does face to face (pp. 116–117, 120).
- Starting routine: write the headline, then subhead, then a caption for the photo you'd use, then the first sentence, checking each pulls into the next (p. 116).
- Map the finished copy as a block diagram that flows one way, downward (pp. 118–119). A sample order for a high-ticket item: excitement and fun, what makes it different, how it works, build quality, justifying the price (compare with other purchases of similar cost, list uses), lasting value, service and reliability, then the request to order (pp. 118–120).
- Axiom 13 (p. 120), paraphrased: present ideas in a logical flow that anticipates and answers questions as if asked in person.
- Before writing, restate the problem several ways (not "sell this" but "make it easy and fun to buy"), list several big ideas, pick the best one or two, then incubate (pp. 120–121).
- Answer objections frankly. A standout paragraph (e.g. bold type for the guarantee) can anchor the flow (pp. 117, 121).
- It is fine to pattern your copy on the structure and tone of a strong ad you admire, without copying it (pp. 121–122).

### Ch 17. The Editing Process (pp. 124–131)

- Axiom 14 (p. 125), paraphrased: editing means saying exactly what you mean in the fewest words while keeping the emotional feel.
- Tools: cut, rearrange, substitute, combine, add. A worked example cut a short paragraph by about 40% (from 59 to 36 words) with nothing lost (pp. 125–127).
- Specific moves: drop throat-clearing lead-ins; vary sentence length for rhythm; merge sentences that repeat a subject; delete words that add nothing (even a lone "the"); reorder thoughts so the emotional point comes first and the explanation follows (pp. 126–127).
- Fewer words make copy look less imposing and speed the reader down the slide (p. 126).
- Some drafts need ten rewrites, some almost none; experience reduces the editing needed (pp. 127–128).
- Practice by trimming filler phrases in magazine articles (p. 128).
- Let copy rest (overnight or a few days, or at least a short break) before a final edit (pp. 129–130).
- Have a skilled proofreader check grammar and spelling, but keep your stylistic choices where no rule is broken. He prefers minimal optional commas (pp. 130–131).
- Errors damage trust: readers assume sloppy writing means a sloppy business, and some will write to complain (pp. 130–131).

### How to apply
(Invented illustrations from different kinds of business.)
- First sentence: short, easy, intriguing. A home-gym equipment page opens "Most people quit by week three." The next line has to be read.
- Slippery slide on a long sales page for an online course: each paragraph ends on a reason to read the next; the price and terms come only after desire is built.
- Seeds of curiosity: an e-mail for a new phone case ends a paragraph with "But that's not the reason it sold out last spring."
- Objections answered where they arise: a mattress brand raises "what if it's too firm?" itself and answers with its trial period.
- Personal, conversational tone: a local dentist's page reads like the dentist speaking to one nervous patient, not like a brochure.
- Specific claims over general: "cuts prep time from 40 minutes to 12" beats "saves time".
- Editing: cut a first draft by a third or more; read it aloud; check every sentence earns the next.

# 2. The Adweek Copywriting Handbook, Part B (Sections Two to Four)

Paraphrased technique notes in my own words, not the book's text. Page refs are PDF page numbers (the printed page number is roughly 23 lower). Part A (pp. 1–135) is covered in a separate file.

### Ch 18. Powerful Copy Elements Explained (pp. 136–153)

Sugarman's 23 copy elements, meant as a checklist to run over every piece of copy.

- Legibility first: in his source tests, serif body type beat sans serif about 5 to 1 on comprehension. Fancy display type that is hard to read has no value (p. 136).
- First sentence short and easy. The second sentence matters almost as much. The first couple of paragraphs exist to build momentum and a buying mood, so hold back the facts and features until the reader is committed (p. 137).
- Paragraph headings make long copy look less heavy. Their wording barely matters (his test with nonsense headings still worked). Place them mid-column, not at the very top or bottom, and never side by side (pp. 137–138).
- Explanation depth runs against complexity: explain a complicated product simply, and give a simple product an in-depth, detailed treatment (his smoke-detector ad went into the internals and sold well at a higher price). Have outsiders read the draft and answer every question they ask (pp. 138–139).
- Lead with what is new or different about this offer compared with the rest of the market (p. 139).
- Technical explanation builds trust even when readers skip it. It signals that you did the homework and surveyed the whole category before choosing this one. Become a real expert first (e.g. the grapefruit grader rejecting fruit for tiny defects) (pp. 139–141).
- Anticipate objections: raise the obvious ones yourself, early, in plain words (he called a thermostat the ugliest on the market), then resolve each honestly. Resolving objections is one of the "major" elements; headings are minor (pp. 141–142, 152).
- Know your audience's sensitivities (gender etc.) so a careless line doesn't offend part of the market (pp. 142–143).
- Clarity: short common words. Clichés make copy sound like stale agency work, so cut them. Rhythm: vary sentence length unpredictably, and use lists of three (pp. 143–144).
- Service: for costly or hard-to-return items, deal with after-sale service up front. A weak point can be turned into a selling point with a long warranty, a loaner, or a pickup (pp. 144–147).
- Physical facts: give every dimension, weight and limitation. Any missing fact gives the buyer an excuse to wait (p. 147).
- Trial period: when buyers can't touch the product, offer at least a month (two is better). Longer trials lower pressure and returns (pp. 147–148).
- Price comparison establishes value. It must be scrupulously fair. If you are dearer, show the extra features; if cheaper, stress value (pp. 148–149).
- Testimonials: credible and relevant to the product. Variants are the "reverse testimonial" (a rival pays a celebrity, so we pass the savings on), the person in the street, and unsolicited letters. Always genuine (pp. 149–150).
- Price display: make a bargain price big. Underplay an expensive price, but never hide it, and put it where a scanning eye finds it the moment the reader wonders (pp. 150–151).
- Close sequence: an offer summary near the end ("so here's my offer"), then make ordering easy, then ask plainly for the order (pp. 151–152).
- Avoid saying too much. This is the most common beginner fault. Ask "is there a simpler way to say this?" and expect to cut 50–80% of a first draft (p. 151).

### Ch 19. The Psychological Triggers (pp. 154–215)

Thirty-one triggers. With the 10 graphic and 23 copy elements they make a 64-point review list (p. 154).

- Involvement/ownership: get the reader to imagine holding and using the product, as if it were already theirs ("when you get it, try..."). Involvement devices (stickers, puzzles) can multiply response. His spelling-computer ad gave $2 off for every misspelled word the reader found. Readers spent ages hunting, found only about half, and he earned more (pp. 154–157).
- Honesty is the most important trigger. Readers are sharper than you think. State the flaw up front and explain why it is minor; frankness lifts response (pp. 157–158).
- Integrity and credibility: the ad shows who the writer is, so keep it clean, not shouty. Give a reason for an unusually low price. Exaggeration, clichés and unresolved objections all destroy belief. Borrow credibility from a respected medium, known brands, a fitting celebrity, and a solid company name and address (pp. 159–161).
- Value and proof of value: compare with products that have similar features. Teaching the reader the product's true worth is the same as lowering its price. People buy on emotion and justify with logic, so supply the logic (pp. 161–162).
- Justify the purchase: give a rational reason (savings, health, "you've earned it"). The higher the price, the more justification is needed (p. 162).
- Greed: give more than expected for the price. Too low a price needs a reason, or it reads as junk (pp. 162–163).
- Authority: in every ad, state some claim of size, specialty or expertise, even a small firm's ("the hardest-working team in..."). Keep repeating it, because new readers arrive all the time. Reassure buyers they're choosing well, e.g. by calling it one of your best-sellers (pp. 163–166).
- Satisfaction conviction: an offer so generous the reader thinks "they must really believe in this". Examples: no time limit on returns, return pickup at the seller's cost, or a refund with interest if the member never buys. In one test this alone doubled response, while a headline change gave about +20%. Place it near the end, and make it answer a likely objection beyond expectation (pp. 166–169).
- Nature of product / nature of prospect: each product has one most dramatic, fitting way to present it (fun, serious, reliable). Learn the real emotional reasons prospects buy, and match the two (pp. 169–172).
- Current fads and timing: use the vocabulary and hot categories of the moment, and reframe a product to fit them (a commemorative necklace sold as jewelry). Get in early and get out after the peak. Timing is unknowable, so test (pp. 172–176).
- Linking: tie the new thing to something the reader already understands. Examples: a smoke detector sold as "The Nose", a walkie-talkie sold as a "pocket CB", an additive sold as "a tune-up in a pill". Never link to something illegal or unsavoury (pp. 176–179).
- Consistency: make the first commitment small and easy. Once someone buys, they tend to buy again. Offer an add-on right after the order; he says it was accepted more than half the time on the phone (pp. 179–181).
- Harmonize with the market first: give buyers what they already want (the Bobby Darin story), and earn the right to do your own thing later (pp. 181–184).
- Desire to belong / desire to collect: buyers want to identify with existing owners, so publicize who your customers are. Collectors and past buyers are the best list for more of the same (pp. 184–187).
- Curiosity: his number one reason direct response works. Don't show or tell everything. The BluBlocker ads never showed the view through the lens (pp. 187–189).
- Urgency: "let me think about it" usually means no sale. Give a true reason to act now (limited stock, price rising, deadline, bonus for quick orders). Never fake it. Urgency belongs at the end; the beginning and the end are the two critical spots (pp. 189–191).
- Fear (of loss, of missing out) is used carefully. Instant gratification: promise fast delivery and over-deliver (pp. 191–192).
- Exclusivity, rarity or uniqueness: make the buyer feel part of a small, envied group (pp. 193–194).
- Simplicity: simple copy, positioning and offer. One men's watch outpulled a nine-choice watch ad about 3 to 1. A plain "one price, lasts a month" offer beat a complex dosage offer. Show the range later to people who have already bought (pp. 195–198).
- Human relationships and storytelling: put the product in human terms. Use a byline, first person, light humour, a hand holding the item for scale. Every element should resonate together (his tuning-fork image). Stories hold attention and bond; good subjects are how the product was discovered, or the writer's own experience (pp. 199–203).
- Mental engagement: let readers work out a conclusion themselves (e.g. state a retail price and what jewelers pay, and let them infer the markup). Over-explaining feels patronising (pp. 203–206).
- Guilt / reciprocity: give so much useful information and entertainment that the reader feels they owe you (pp. 206–207).
- Specificity: exact numbers and facts read as expert and true ("92% of new dentists", not "most dentists") (pp. 207–208).
- Familiarity: familiar words, formats and brands attract. Don't drop a working campaign because you're bored of it; only sales say it's tired. A one-word change can double response (pp. 208–211).
- Hope: imply a future benefit through stories and testimonials. Never promise a measurable result by a date, because unmet promises breed anger (pp. 211–215).

### Ch 20. Selling a Cure, Not Prevention (pp. 216–220)

- Prevention is a hard sell because people assume the bad thing won't happen to them. A cure sells easily, and at a higher price (p. 216).
- The best prospect for a preventive product is someone who was just hit by the problem (burglary, a death in the family), because for them it has become a cure (pp. 216–217).
- Ask of every product: is this a preventive or a cure? Then reposition it as a cure, or lead with its curative effects (a fuel additive led with "stops knocking, better mileage" and underplayed the engine protection) (pp. 218–220).

### Ch 21. Rating Your Writing Level (pp. 222–225)

- Use the Fog index. Take a sample of about 100 words, find the average sentence length, and add the percentage of words with three or more syllables (leave out proper names, compound words, and words that only reach three syllables through -ed or -es). Multiply by 0.4 to get a school grade level (pp. 222–223).
- Lower grade means a wider audience. Bestsellers sit around grade 8–10, quality news magazines around 11. His best ad opened near grade 5 (pp. 222–224).
- Test several 100-word blocks for consistency. Use it as a check, not an obsession, and adjust to the audience (p. 224).

### Ch 22. Seven Steps to Writing Great Copy (pp. 226–232)

1. Become an expert on the product.
2. Know the prospect and what motivates them.
3. Write the headline and subheadline: a short headline that sparks curiosity, a supporting subhead of about 16 words, then a short first sentence.
4. Write the draft fast, emptying your head without worrying about grammar.
5. Edit: correct, cut unneeded words, tighten.
6. Let it incubate; step away, ideally overnight.
7. Take a final look, and repeat steps 5–6 as needed (pp. 226–228).

### Ch 23. The Lazy Man's Way to Riches (pp. 234–241)

- Open with very short sentences in which the writer identifies with the reader's situation, then build curiosity before revealing the offer (pp. 234–236).
- An early satisfaction conviction: the seller won't cash the payment for 31 days (p. 236).
- Modest, relatable proof of success, plus third-party proof (an accountant's statement, a bank reference) (pp. 237–239).
- Broaden appeal by listing what isn't required (education, capital, youth...), each with an example (p. 238).
- A "what have you got to lose?" close. The coupon restates the offer and guarantee in the buyer's own voice (pp. 239–240).

### Ch 24. A Fluke of Nature (pp. 242–249)

- Short curiosity headline plus explanatory subhead. The writer stripped out agency-sounding lines and kept the grower's homespun voice; authenticity beat polish (pp. 243, 247).
- An identity word in the opening line (a farmer) carries instant credibility. Then rarity, with how few fruit qualify, and a detailed selection process to prove expertise (pp. 243–246).
- Low-risk offer: a sample shipment you try before paying and can return at the seller's cost, followed by continuity you can skip or cancel. Truthful urgency through limited membership (pp. 246–248).

### Ch 25. Lingerie for Men (pp. 250–255)

- Story opening in large type. The merchandise is woven into the narrative so the reader pictures it (pp. 250–251).
- Raise the awkward objection as a heading (are men embarrassed?), answer it honestly, broaden to all men and flatter them (pp. 251–252).
- Critique: the ending was weak, with no return promise. He would have added a byline and credited the catalog fee to a first order. The ending is where the decision happens (pp. 252–254).

### Ch 26. The More You Learn (pp. 256–259)

- An ad that tried to reassure without naming the elephant (a plane's safety record) felt hollow. The rewrite named the bad news in the headline and subhead, then showed what was fixed. Always raise the embarrassing objection, then resolve it.

### Ch 27. A More Stimulating Way (pp. 260–261)

- A dense all-caps headline, no subhead and a long first sentence lose readers. A story of one person who switched and got a dramatic benefit would have sold better.

### Ch 28. Magic Baloney (pp. 262–265)

- Reversal opening: the copy promises to tear the product apart, admitting the ugly case, the silly name and the missing display. Then it disarms each objection with tests, warranty and buy-back. Hit the number one purchase barrier (installation) early.

### Ch 29. Pet Plane (pp. 266–268)

- A one-off big-ticket sale with a personal story. Specifics (hours, inspection intervals), a reason for selling, self-deprecating humour, and a comparison with the new price as proof of value.

### Ch 30. Mail Order Mansion (pp. 270–273)

- A story ad that builds curiosity ("why was I there?"), uses sensory detail, offers a trial and terms with humour, and adds a cheaper fallback offer in a PS. It got publicity but no sale; boldness carries risk.

### Ch 31. Hungarian Conspiracy (pp. 274–277)

- A tongue-in-cheek, news-style story that holds back what the product is until late. The offer comes only in the final paragraph, so curiosity carries the reader all the way down.

### Ch 32. Vision Breakthrough (pp. 278–280)

- His biggest winner. Sunglasses were repositioned as a vision breakthrough, using a true story about a friend and a simple science explanation. He never showed the view through the lens, so the reader had to buy to satisfy curiosity. "Do they work?" was answered with a no-risk trial, and value was proven against expensive European glasses.

### Ch 33. Gold Space Chains (pp. 282–285)

- A story premise (a persistent friend's suggestion) justifies an off-category product. "Compare it after you get it" invites a price check. Lesson in tone: one careless line offended part of the audience.

### Ch 34. Consumers Hero (pp. 286–289)

- Fit the voice to the market: a consumer-advocate voice, not an expert voice. Transparent economics with exact figures, numbered reasons, a long warranty as proof of pride, and a refund plus interest if the member never buys.

### Ch 35. Nautilus Spelling Sale (pp. 290–293)

- A closeout without public discounting: a listed price with money off per misspelled word found. This is an involvement device and a hidden discount in one. Honest limits, full specs and humour throughout. Break rules when the concept justifies it.

### Ch 36. Writing for Different Media (pp. 300–335)

- Same principles everywhere; only the weighting changes by medium (p. 298).
- Catalogs: the cover is the headline, so lead with the most dramatic item. A signed letter from the owner with a photo humanises the business. Each item needs a short enticing headline, a subhead and every key fact. Match the look to the price point, give important items more space, and show the phone number on every page (pp. 300–302).
- Package inserts and bounce-backs: buyers read what comes in the box, so include the next offer (p. 303).
- Direct mail: the most personal format. Write "I", sign it, and let the letter do the selling while the brochure supports it. The PS is among the most-read lines, so put a clincher there. Your own recent buyers are the best list. The envelope is the headline; a plain or personal-looking one gets opened. Test variants (pp. 303–306).
- Newspapers, billboards: a mail-order ad should pay back on its first run, while retail needs frequency. Billboards get one short idea and a graphic (pp. 306–310).
- Press releases: write news, not puff. Put the 5 Ws early, use attributed quotes, aim at that publication's readers, and use a topical hook or linking. Think like the editor (pp. 310–312).
- Radio: one core message per spot, a memorable vanity number, and a strong curiosity or news-style opening. Sponsor content that fits the product (e.g. weather for sunglasses) (pp. 312–315).
- TV direct response: an impulse price, value that feels too good to be true, one simple offer with any bonus only at the end, a reason to call now, mass appeal, and a bonus everyone wants (pp. 315–317).
- Back-end and after-sale: upsell right after the first order (deluxe version, a second unit at a discount, continuity). Follow up through the channel they bought in. Phone scripts build a run of yeses before "how many would you like?" (pp. 319–321).
- Internet: a small firm can look big, testing is cheap and fast, and the opt-in list is the asset (pp. 321–323).
- Email: the subject line is the envelope and headline, so keep it brief and make the sender or topic clear. Avoid spammy words. Keep the email short and link to the longer message. Send useful material regularly so opens become a habit, then sell (pp. 323–325).
- Long web sales letter: length is fine if the reader wants the info and the copy is a slippery slide. Put buy buttons throughout, including near the top, and turn headings into links to the order page. Use real-name testimonials (audio/video where possible), a natural demo video and your own photo (pp. 325–327).
- Web store: an offer understood in seconds, same-day shipping, clear returns, and few clicks to buy. The common failure is thin product descriptions: say what it's made of, where it's from and why it's better (pp. 332–334).
- Blogs, launches, viral: write regularly; topical names help search. A launch-day push with partner bonuses that buyers would actually pay for. Shareable content needs conversion copy on the landing page (pp. 329–332).

### Appendices A–C (pp. 340–347)

- A: Don't assume constraints that aren't real; history is full of confident "can't be done" verdicts that were wrong (pp. 340–341).
- B: The cliffhanger in an earlier chapter proved the point: compelling copy makes people do things they normally wouldn't, like skipping to the back of a book (pp. 342–343).

### How to apply
(Invented illustrations from different kinds of business.)
- Copy elements in order: a skincare product's page uses headline, subhead, photo with caption, first sentence, then copy that answers questions in the order a buyer asks them.
- Captions carry a sale on their own: under a product photo of a backpack, "Fits a 16-inch laptop and a day's food, under 1 kg" does more than "Our backpack".
- The incubation step: research a meal-delivery service thoroughly, step away, then write; the big idea often arrives after the break.
- Price and value: a freelance designer's rate is broken down into cost per finished asset and compared with hiring in-house.
- Ownership and involvement: a gardening tool's copy puts the reader in the yard on a Saturday morning using it.
- One clear choice: a software pricing page highlights one recommended plan instead of five equal ones.
- Satisfaction conviction near the close: a subscription box offers to refund the first box if the reader is not delighted, and says why it can afford to.

# 3. Advertising Secrets of the Written Word (1998)

Paraphrased technique notes, not the book's text. Page refs are PDF page markers of the scanned edition (printed page numbers run roughly 10 lower). This is the 1998 original of the later Adweek Copywriting Handbook (2007); where a chapter matches the Adweek version it is kept short here, and the effort goes into what is different or extra (see "Differences" near the end).

### Introduction (pp. 14–20)
- The whole book grew out of a five-day live seminar the author ran from 1977 to 1988; several graduates went on to build well-known direct-response businesses.
- His working definition of the job: copy exists to get someone to swap money they worked for in exchange for what you sell.
- He uses the print ad as the reference medium because it is the hardest (no sound, no motion, many rival messages); anything that works there transfers to other media.

### Ch 1. General Knowledge (pp. 23–26)
- Strong writers are curious generalists, good listeners, and unafraid of failure; a mistake is stored experience you will reuse.
- Lateral-thinking drill: draw three random words and force them into a solution to your marketing problem (his example: selling his own plane using "farm", "salesman", "compassion").
- Owning the business, and being responsible for every word's result, produces the best direct-response writers.

### Ch 2. Specific Knowledge (pp. 27–33)
- Dig until you find the one fact that becomes the concept (two days in a watch lab yielded the laser-sealed-capsule angle).
- Know the buyer; it helps when you are a typical buyer yourself.
- Understand the product's nature: a burglar alarm sold on reliability and easy installation, with an astronaut endorsement and no crime-statistics scare, kept selling for years because people clipped the ad and ordered later.
- A weakness can be reframed: a walkie-talkie that happened to sit on a CB channel became the "Pocket CB" (about 250,000 sold).

### Ch 3. Practice, Practice, Practice (pp. 34–36)
- Copy is written communication of facts plus emotions; just start writing, expect a rough first draft, and get good through polishing. Axiom 1: copy is the sum of your experience, specific knowledge and mental processing, aimed at a sale.

### Ch 4. The Purpose of All the Graphic Elements (pp. 37–38)
- Ten graphic elements (headline, subhead, photo, caption, copy, paragraph headings, logo, price, response device, layout) all serve one end: getting the first sentence read (Axiom 2).

### Ch 5. The First Sentence (pp. 39–43)
- Make the opener tiny and easy, almost incomplete; each sentence's only job is to get the next one read (Axiom 3). No need to pile in benefits early.

### Ch 6. Creating the Perfect Buying Environment (pp. 44–47)
- Like a salesperson choosing the right room, the layout and first paragraphs set the mood (Axiom 4). The art-gallery story: a quiet back room, music and spotlights made a $2,000 painting feel right.
- Match the environment to the offer: discount goods look like a discount ad; expensive goods look refined and calm. Different brands of his used deliberately different looks.

### Ch 7. Resonating with the Reader (pp. 48–53)
- Get early agreement with true, checkable statements so the reader keeps nodding; one "no" or "I don't believe that" loses them (Axiom 5).
- Mirror the prospect: he won a magazine's ad space deal by mocking up an ad in the prospect's own house style; speak the buyer's language.

### Ch 8. The Slippery Slide (pp. 54–61)
- Every element should pull the reader to the next; readers who get through roughly the first quarter tend to finish (Axiom 6).
- Extra here: keep a clipping file of odd news items and open an ad with one, tied loosely or tightly to the product; he even admitted in one ad that the opening story was invented, and it still worked.
- Opening by criticising your own product (ugly case, silly name) creates a pull to find out why you are selling it anyway.

### Ch 9. Assumed Constraints (pp. 62–66)
- Do not accept limits that are not real (Axiom 7). He lists things he was told could not be sold by mail and then sold (a $240,000 plane, sunglasses, a costly pinball machine).

### Ch 10. Seeds of Curiosity (pp. 67–71)
- End paragraphs with short hooks ("but there's more") and promise early a payoff you deliver later; do not overuse (Axiom 8).
- He demonstrates it by leaving a story unfinished and completing it in an appendix. The story's lesson: a failed mailing came from the wrong list (store buyers, not mail-order buyers), weak copy and testing far too large a quantity first.

### Ch 11. Copy as Emotion (pp. 72–77)
- Three emotion principles: every word carries feeling; a good ad is an emotional outpouring; people buy on emotion and justify with logic.
- Sell the scene, not the spec (a phone-number calculator sold through the stress of a phone-booth moment). Small word swaps change response (a classic test: a plainer verb for "repair" lifted response by about a fifth). Do not alter facts to get a warmer word.

### Ch 12. Selling the Concept, Not the Product (pp. 78–81)
- Sell the concept (positioning, big idea), never the bare product (Axiom 9). A genuinely new product is its own concept until rivals arrive.
- Extra detail here: the same copy at three price points framed the same item three ways (serious gear, upscale walkie-talkie, toy). Two unrelated products can be bundled under one concept. A product name can carry the concept.

### Ch 13. The Incubation Process (pp. 82–85)
- Load up on facts, jot headlines and reasons to buy, picture the result, then stop and do something pleasant; the answer surfaces later. Time pressure helps if balanced with time away (Axiom 10).

### Ch 14. How Much Copy Should You Write? (pp. 86–91)
- Copy is too long only if it fails to get the action (Axiom 11); match the length of a full in-person pitch.
- More copy is needed for higher prices and unusual items. A very low price can also need more copy to be believed. Short copy works when the setting (catalog, photos, low price) does the selling.
- Interest varies with the product's life cycle: novelty-stage products earn long reading, while commodity-stage products need less.

### Ch 15. The Art of Personal Communication (pp. 92–97)
- Every message goes from one person to one person (Axiom 12); a byline lets you write "I" and "you" naturally in print.
- Extra example: a bargain club's folksy, small-company letters (staff names, reused envelopes, playful membership cards) made honest readers feel personally connected. A catalog's opening letter licenses first-person voice throughout.

### Ch 16. The Copy Sequence (pp. 98–104)
- Answer each question in the very next sentence, the way it would come up face to face (Axiom 13).
- Block-diagram the flow (his pinball example): excitement, then what is different, how it works, build quality, justification of price, lasting value, service, then ask for the order.
- Restate the problem several ways until one phrasing makes the product come alive; "patterning" means borrowing the format and feel of an admired ad without copying it.

### Ch 17. The Editing Process (pp. 105–112)
- Say it with the fewest words while keeping the emotion (Axiom 14). In one example, about 40% of the words were cut and nothing was lost.
- Cut lead-ins that end in "that". Vary sentence length. Merge sentences, drop needless words and filler phrases, and reorder so the pleasure comes before the reasons. Let the draft sit overnight.
- Extra here: give the final draft to a professional editor for grammar and spelling, but keep your own style. He skips the serial comma to save space. Errors cost credibility with some readers.

### Ch 18. Powerful Copy Elements Explained (pp. 115–131)
Section Two opens with a "57 points" checklist: 10 graphic elements, 23 copy elements and 24 triggers. The 23 copy elements match the Adweek list. Points worth keeping:
- Use a serif typeface for body text, because readability comes before style. Paragraph headings break up the copy and work even when they say little; place them mid-column.
- Explain a complex product simply, and a simple product in technical depth (gold contacts in a smoke detector justified a higher price). A technical passage signals expertise even to readers who skim it.
- Raise objections yourself, then resolve them honestly. Cover service up front for costly or hard-to-return items: a 5-year warranty with a loaner watch and courier pickup turned an industry reliability problem into a selling point.
- Give every physical fact (weight, size, limits). Each missing fact is a reason not to buy, and most people who miss one never call to ask.
- Offer a trial period of a month or more; longer trials bring fewer returns. Price comparisons must be exact.
- Testimonials need a credible, well-matched endorser. "Reverse testimonial": point out that the rival paid a famous golfer while your own president endorses yours, and pass the saving on.
- Show the price big when it is a bargain and play it down (without hiding it) when it is high. Then summarise the offer, make ordering easy and ask for the order at the end.
- The biggest beginner mistake is saying too much; ask whether there is a simpler way, and often 50–80% can go.

### Ch 19. The Psychological Triggers (pp. 131–175)
In this edition there are 24 triggers. The Adweek handbook has 31, and its extra seven are listed under Differences. Most match Adweek; points to note:
- Involvement/ownership: get the reader holding or using the product in their head. Response devices that feel silly (yes-stickers) still lift response. Planted spelling errors with a discount per error found kept readers in an ad for over an hour and raised revenue.
- Honesty is called the single most important point of all 57. State a flaw early, then explain why it is minor.
- Credibility can be borrowed from the medium, the brand, the company name or an impressive address. A cheap price needs a stated reason (bulk buy, closeout).
- Greed: a "pricing error" ad (the price is going up, buy now at the old one) out-pulled the original.
- Authority: say something about your size or standing in every ad, even a small claim ("hardest-working team"), and keep repeating it for new readers.
- Satisfaction conviction: make an offer so generous the reader thinks "they must really believe in it". In one test a headline change lifted response 20% and a price change did little, but a refund-plus-interest promise doubled response.
- Fads and timing: ride a fad early and leave at its peak. External news can kill or rescue a campaign, so test everything.
- Simplicity: one watch in one colour out-pulled nine choices 3 to 1 in a split test. A plain "$25 a month" beat a two-step dosing offer.
- Familiarity: never drop a campaign because you are bored with it, only when sales fall. One word changed in a thousand can double response.
- Hope: do not promise a measurable outcome by a specific date. Let testimonials and your own experience imply it, or one missed result destroys trust.
- Also covered: belonging, collecting (send a free display holder with the first item), curiosity (say less and show less), urgency (only real reasons, placed at the end), instant gratification (state shipping speed), exclusivity, guilt (give a lot of value first), specificity (exact figures read as expert) and human relationships (every element "tuned" to the same pitch).

### Ch 20. Getting the Mind to Work (pp. 176–179)
- Axiom 15 in this edition: the more the reader's mind works to reach a conclusion on its own, the more satisfying the copy feels. Spelling everything out bores readers and talks down to them.
- Leave the profitable conclusion unstated: stating a well-known product's retail price, its trade price and its shortage let readers work out on their own that sellers were making a lot.
- A literal photo can kill an ad: adding a jogger image and colour to an exercise-machine ad made it flop. When editing, ask whether you are being too obvious.

### Ch 21. Selling a Cure, Not Prevention (pp. 180–184)
- People pay little for prevention and a lot for a cure (Axiom 16). Find buyers in a cure moment (a neighbour was just burgled), or reframe the benefits as cures.
- For a fuel additive, lead with the fixes (knocking gone, better mileage, passing emissions) and play down the "protects your engine" angle.

### Ch 22. Telling a Story (pp. 185–188)
- Axiom 17 in this edition: a story sells, sets the mood and pulls the reader deep into the copy while building an emotional bond. His biggest campaigns were stories, and one ad that was nearly all story (a mansion for sale) still drew huge attention.
- First person works best, third person works too, and a fairy-tale "discovery" opening can run for years.

### Ch 23. Rating Your Writing Level (pp. 189–191)
- Gunning Fog Index: take 100–125 words, add the average sentence length to the percentage of words with three or more syllables, then multiply by 0.4. Bestsellers read at grade 8–10, and his best ad opening scored about 5. Lower scores reach more people, but do not obsess over the number.

### Ch 24. The Lazy Man's Way to Riches (pp. 196–203)
- A short, identifying opening; a modest, believable picture of success; and an early guarantee not to cash the check for 31 days.
- Widen the audience with a run of "you don't need X" lines, each with a real example. An accountant's sworn statement and a bank reference build proof, and the coupon restates the offer in the reader's own skeptical voice.

### Ch 25. A Fluke of Nature (pp. 204–211)
- A farmer's own story beat his agency's ads. The word "farmer" signalled honesty, rarity was backed by numbers (only a small percentage of fruit qualifies), and his grading standards made a simple product sound technical.
- The offer was a no-risk taste test (keep it or return it at his cost) leading into a club, with honest urgency (limited memberships). The ad kept a big, growing business sounding small and personal.

### Ch 26. Lingerie for Men (pp. 212–216)
- A short headline that hides the premise, a story opening and the awkward objection raised as a subhead. The real offer was a paid catalog (two-step lead generation).
- The flaw he points to: a weak close with no guarantee and no credit of the catalog fee against a first order. The decision happens at the end.

### Ch 27. The More You Learn (pp. 217–220)
- An airliner ad after a crash praised safety testing without naming the crash, so it rang hollow. His rewrite names the disaster first, explains it, then reassures. Raise the most damaging objection first.

### Ch 28. A More Stimulating Way (pp. 221–222)
- An agency ad about boredom was boring: hard-to-read all-caps headline, no subhead or caption, long first sentence. A customer success story would have sold it.

### Ch 29. Magic Baloney (pp. 223–226)
- Knock the product first, then discover its merits and work through the buyer's objections in order (installation was the main barrier). Build up the small maker's credibility, add a strong guarantee (buy-back if no savings within a year) and give a tax-credit justification.

### Ch 30. Pet Plane (pp. 227–229)
- A $240,000 plane sold from one magazine ad; it only needed one buyer. The features were woven into a personal story with a byline and gentle self-mocking humour that kept the serious message intact.

### Ch 31. Mail Order Mansion (pp. 230–233)
- A multimillion-dollar house offered in an airline-magazine ad, with a paid video as the secondary offer and even a 30-day return privilege. The house did not sell, but the ad drew huge publicity. A calculated stunt.

### Ch 32. Hungarian Conspiracy (pp. 234–237)
- A catalog item sold through a mock news story telling readers not to buy it, with the product revealed only at the end. Pushing the envelope can work when curiosity carries the reader.

### Ch 33. Vision Breakthrough (pp. 238–240)
- Sunglasses positioned as a vision breakthrough, through a discovery story, a byline, a technical explanation of blue light and comparison with $100–200 brands. A 30-day "see a dramatic difference or return them" guarantee launched a brand.

### Ch 34. Gold Space Chains (pp. 241–243)
- A humorous story was invented to justify an off-category product. He admits it offended women readers and that a gold-price spike, not the copy, explains much of its sales. Watch tone and do not credit copy for luck.

### Ch 35. Consumers Hero (pp. 244–247)
- A consumer-advocate voice in plain language, with a cost breakdown showing how a refurbished item could sell so cheaply. Open margins build belief. A refundable membership fee with interest worked as the satisfaction conviction. A good writer can switch between upscale and downscale voices for different businesses.

### Ch 36. Nautilus Spelling Sale (pp. 248–251)
- The supplier forbade discounts, so he set a price above retail and took money off for every planted misspelling found. Rules, joke disclaimers and a no-purchase entry route made it a game; readers spent hours on it and margin rose. Full physical facts and a 30-day trial were still included.

### A Note and Epilogue (pp. 252–256)
- Practice is what makes the pen powerful. Looking back, he wishes he had tested more.
- Three traits he credits: making complex things simple, persistence, and being an originator who creates rather than a compiler who rearranges others' ideas.

### Appendices (pp. 257–299)
- A–C: more examples of false limits, the payoff of the curiosity story, and a summary of the 17 axioms, 3 emotion principles and the 10 + 23 + 24 checklists.
- D: a recommended-reading list on selling and direct marketing.
- E: 16 classic catalog ads with short commentary (most are not in the Adweek edition):
  - A child's calculator pitched with humour to executives flopped: the humour did not fit the product.
  - An exquisitely written gadget ad sold almost nothing: great copy cannot save a product nobody wants.
  - A knock-off watch ad openly admitted the copy and explained why it cost less, and it succeeded.
  - Another ad flowed out almost unedited and won publicity but flopped.
  - Silly premises carried real products: a gadget "for the Mafia" with practical uses, "pickle power" batteries, a charter space-shuttle fantasy that sold posters, a mailbox put through a nuclear-blast hypothetical to dramatise toughness, a playful riff on an odd product name.
  - A cartoon headline about a garbage truck led into a technical lesson.
  - Shared theme: entertaining catalogs built readers who looked forward to the next issue.

### How to apply
(Invented illustrations from different kinds of business.)
- Sell the cure, not the prevention: a pest-control company leads with "ants in the kitchen gone in 48 hours" rather than "protect your home"; a backup service leads with "recover the file you just deleted".
- Give unusual prices a stated reason: a budget course explains it is self-paced with no live calls; a premium coffee explains the small farm, the hand sorting and the roast-to-order delay.
- A byline voice: a short signed note from the founder or lead practitioner lets a whole page speak person to person.
- Social posts and blog openers: start from a curious true detail (what a repair shop found inside a returned laptop) and let readers draw the conclusion; avoid captions that explain the joke.
- App store text: one clear choice and a plain call to action; cut feature lists by half on the edit pass and check the reading level.
- Lead generation (a calculator, a checklist, a free consult): close with a reason to act now (a real limit, such as consultation slots per month) and a credit toward the first paid service, which fixes the weak "maybe later" close.
