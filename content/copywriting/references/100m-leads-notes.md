# Notes: $100M Leads: How to Get Strangers To Want To Buy Your Stuff (Alex Hormozi, 2023)

Paraphrased technique notes for the lead-generation and outreach section of the copywriting skill. These are summaries in our own words, not the book's text. Page numbers are PDF page indexes of the source file.

## Ch 1. How I Got Here (pp. 14–30)
- Origin story: a cash crisis was solved by a mix of paid ads, posting client success stories in communities, and paying customers for referrals; lead flow was the bottleneck.
- The book is ordered from the cheapest, lowest-skill methods (no money, no audience) to the most complex, so a beginner can get the first five clients and a large business can remember the basics it stopped doing.

## Ch 2. The Problem This Book Solves (pp. 31–36)
- A business grows in only two ways: more customers, or customers worth more. This book is about the first: more leads, better leads, cheaper leads, reliably.
- Doubling engaged leads roughly doubles the business with no other change.

## Ch 3. Leads Alone Aren't Enough (pp. 40–42)
- A lead is simply someone you can contact. An engaged lead is someone who has shown interest (replied, opted in, booked, followed and responded).
- Advertising's real output is engaged leads, not impressions or contact lists. Measure that.

## Ch 4. Engage Your Leads: Offers and Lead Magnets (pp. 43–72)
- You can advertise the core offer directly (often best first) or a lead magnet: a free or cheap offer that gets people to raise their hand. A strong lead magnet solves one narrow problem completely and in doing so reveals the next problem, which the paid offer solves (p. 51–52).
- Seven-step build process (p. 50): pick the narrow problem and the person; decide how to solve it; decide how to deliver it; test the name; make it easy to consume; make it genuinely valuable; give a clear call to action.
- Three lead magnet types (pp. 53–56): (1) reveal a problem, e.g. a diagnosis, audit, inspection or test that shows what is wrong and how it gets worse; (2) a sample or trial of the core offer, limited by uses or time, best for recurring needs; (3) one step of a multi-step process, e.g. the first stage done for them, or a free calculator/template that also reveals how much work the rest involves.
- Delivery (pp. 57–58): four delivery forms (software/tool, information, service, physical product) across the three types give about a dozen possible lead magnets; rotate and test them.
- Naming (pp. 58–63): the packaging decides engagement; the headline carries most of the value. Test headline, then image, then subheadline. In the book-title tests, adding "how to" beat the imperative version and deleting the word "more" improved it. Test cheaply: polls, ask followers to reply "1" or "2", or just message people.
- Easy to consume (pp. 64–65): offer it in several formats (read, watch, listen) and make it available instantly; easier consumption can multiply engagement.
- Value (pp. 65–66): "give away the secrets, sell the implementation" (p. 65). The free thing should be so good people feel they owe you; most will never buy, but all of them judge your paid work by it.
- Call to action (pp. 66–69): say exactly what to do next and give a reason to do it now. Use real scarcity (limited spots, capacity) and urgency (deadlines, expiring bonuses, cohort start dates). Any reason beats no reason; even a thin "because..." lifts response. Be clear, not clever.
- Even a lead magnet that costs money usually lowers the cost per customer (pp. 69–72).

## Ch 5. The Core Four (Section III intro) (pp. 73–77)
- Warm or cold audience x one-to-one or one-to-many gives the Core Four: warm outreach (1:1 warm), posting free content (1:many warm), cold outreach (1:1 cold), paid ads (1:many cold).
- Not getting enough leads means either not enough skill at a method or not enough volume. Both are fixable.

## Ch 6. #1 Warm Outreach (pp. 78–107)
- Ten-step process (p. 83 onward): build a list of everyone you know; pick the platform where you have the most contacts; personalize; reach out; warm them up; ask for referrals; make the easiest offer; repeat on the next platform; start charging; keep the list warm.
- Reach out to about 100 people a day, up to three times each, once a day for three days or until they reply (pp. 85–86).
- Personalize with a real reason to reach out (something you saw on their profile). Ask for nothing at first.
- When they reply use A-C-A (p. 86–87): Acknowledge (restate what they said), Compliment (tie it to a good trait), Ask a question that steers toward your topic.
- After a few exchanges, ask whether they know anyone with the problem rather than pitching them directly (p. 90); people often say "actually, that's me". Shape: who has [struggle], wants [dream outcome] in [time]; you are taking on a few people free in exchange for feedback and a testimonial.
- Frame the offer with the value equation (p. 88): bigger dream outcome, higher perceived likelihood, shorter time delay, less effort and sacrifice. If they say no, look for hidden costs (time, effort, sacrifice) and ask what would make it worth it (p. 97).
- Pricing ladder (p. 100): give the first five free for testimonials, then raise price in steps every five customers (e.g. deep discount, then smaller discount, then full price).
- Reactivation (p. 102): the short "9-word email", one plain question asking whether they are still looking to get a four-word version of their desire. No images, no links.
- Benchmarks (p. 104): roughly 1 in 5 replies, 1 in 5 of those take the free offer; overall about one customer per 100 warm reach outs. Do it first thing daily, about four hours.

## Ch 7. #2 Post Free Content Part I: Build an Audience (pp. 108–129)
- The asset is the audience, not the posts. It compounds, and it makes every other method work better because people already know you.
- Every piece of content is a unit of Hook, Retain, Reward (p. 116).
- Hook: give a reason to stop. Choose a topic, then a headline, then a format.
- Topics from five time frames (p. 117): far past (life lessons, the story without the scar), recent past (client results, what happened this week), present (document what you are doing now), trending (topical events), manufactured (create an experience to talk about).
- Headlines use news elements (p. 121): recency, relevance, celebrity, proximity, conflict, unusualness, ongoing story. Use at least two at once.
- Format: copy the shape of the best-performing content on that platform for your market, then tune it to hook your ideal audience.
- Retain (pp. 124–126): curiosity keeps people watching. Three tools: lists (state the number up front), steps (ordered, explicit payoff at the end), stories (events with a lesson). Mix them.
- Reward (pp. 126–127): deliver what the hook promised, to the right audience, with high value per second. Nothing is too long, only too boring. Proof of reward is audience growth.

## Ch 8. #2 Post Free Content Part II: Monetize Your Audience (pp. 130–150)
- Give far more than you ask. Commercial TV runs a few minutes of ads per many minutes of content; treat that ratio as a floor, and overgive on growing platforms. Best strategy: give until they ask (p. 132).
- Give in public, ask in private where possible.
- Two ways to ask (pp. 134–135): integrated (a short ask placed at a valuable moment or the end of long content) and intermittent (many pure-give posts, then an occasional ask post). Short-form suits intermittent; long-form suits integrated.
- Offer template shape for an ask: looking for a small number of [avatar] who want [dream outcome] in [time], without [effort], with [guarantee]; reply or book if that sounds fair.
- Scaling (pp. 138–139): depth-then-width (max one platform, then add another) or width-then-depth (repurpose everywhere). Short-form can go up to many posts a day; long-form a few times a week.
- Lessons (pp. 141–144): content often pre-sells buyers (most clients had consumed several long pieces before booking); people need reminding more than teaching; narrow the focus ("puddles, ponds, lakes, oceans", p. 143) — a local business should make local, niche content; keep a "greatest hits" library labeled by the problem each piece solves, and send it before or after sales calls; post manually rather than pre-scheduling.
- Benchmarks (p. 145): track audience size and growth rate monthly; keep inputs consistent.

## Ch 9. Free Goodwill (pp. 151–154)
- A review request done well: explain who the review helps (someone like the reader), say it takes under a minute, give exact step-by-step instructions.

## Ch 10. #3 Cold Outreach (pp. 155–186)
- Cold leads have no trust. Three problems in order: get contact info, know what to say, and repeat until they are ready.
- Build a list: scraping software, list brokers (always test a sample), or manual collection from groups and communities (freshest, least contacted). Start with a first 1,000.
- Personalize (pp. 168–169): one to three facts a friend might know, plus a compliment, ideally showing how their work helped you. People give time to those who know something about them. Batch the research.
- Lead with big, fast value (p. 167): something they would normally pay for, delivered quickly, beats a vague "strategy call". Swapping a generic call for a real piece of the service tripled take-up in one case.
- Keep scripts short (a page or two for calls/chat; email under half a page). Don't tweak before about 100 conversations or several thousand emails.
- Volume: automate delivery (sequences, recorded pieces) and distribution where possible; personalization vs scale is a trade-off, and a small market means personalize everything.
- Follow up more times and in more ways (call about the email you sent). High-ticket deals often need two or three conversations. Re-run old lists every few months; circumstances change.
- Benchmarks (pp. 179–180): calls — 100 calls, ~20% pick up, ~25% take the offer, about 4 engaged leads; email — 100 personalized emails, ~30% open, ~10% reply, aim for ~3% engaged; personal video or voice note DMs — ~20% reply. Success floor: lifetime gross profit at least 3x the cost to acquire.
- Work backward (p. 183): if 100 emails make one customer, decide the customer goal and solve for daily sends and headcount.

## Ch 11. #4 Run Paid Ads Part I: Making an Ad (pp. 187–214)
- A plain ad with a sharp offer can beat polished creative. Paid ads rent attention, so the game is efficiency. Pick a platform you use yourself, can target on, and can afford.
- Targeting: start with your customers (lookalikes), then warm audiences, then cold; narrow targets win early but burn out faster.
- Every ad has three parts: callout, value, call to action. Spend most effort on the first few seconds; each second sells the next.
- Callouts use the cocktail party effect (p. 197). Verbal callouts (p. 198): labels (place + type of person), yes-questions that self-qualify, if-then statements, ridiculous results. Non-verbal (p. 199): contrast (stand out from the feed), likeness (show people who look like the customer), scenes (recognizable places).
- Value: the What-Who-When framework (pp. 204–207). What = the four value drivers and their opposites (dream outcome vs nightmare, likelihood vs risk, speed vs delay, ease vs effort), eight angles. Who = how others (partner, kids, boss, rivals, friends) will see them. When = past, present, future. Combine for endless angles. Lower perceived risk by addressing past failures and showing success of people like them (p. 204).
- Long vs short ads differ only in how many angles they cover; keep the callout and CTA the same (p. 209).
- CTA (pp. 210–213): say exactly what to do; use a short memorable URL; keep the landing page simple (words and image matter most); make it match the ad; use consistency ("you did A, now do B").

## Ch 12. #4 Run Paid Ads Part II: Money Stuff (pp. 215–229)
- Three phases (p. 216): track money (set up tracking before spending), lose money (test with a capped budget), print money (scale winners).
- Measure lifetime gross profit (LTGP) against cost to acquire (CAC); aim for at least 3:1 (p. 220). Two levers: lower CAC or raise LTGP.
- CAC tends to be similar across competitors; winners make more per customer. If your CAC is within a reasonable multiple of the industry average, fix the business model (upsells, retention) rather than the ads.
- Client-financed acquisition (p. 216 onward): make enough gross profit in the first 30 days (via upsells, add-ons) to cover the cost of getting and serving the customer, so growth funds itself.

## Ch 13. Core Four On Steroids: More, Better, New (pp. 230–246)
- Most "saturated" markets aren't. Sequence (p. 233): do more, then do it better, then do something new.
- More: the Rule of 100 (p. 234) — 100 primary advertising actions a day for 100 days (100 warm reach outs, 100 minutes of content creation with at least one post, 100 cold contacts, or $100/100 minutes on ads).
- Better: improve the constraint — the step where the most leads drop off gives the biggest return for the smallest change.
- Test one thing per week per platform; review weekly (keep winners, log results, set the next test). If several tries fail on one step, move to the next constraint.
- New: only when more and better return less than a new placement, then a new platform, then a new Core Four method, in that order.
- Ask: what would stop you getting 10x the leads? If you have more time than money, lean on content and outreach; more money than time, lean on cold outreach staff and ads.

## Ch 14. Get Lead Getters (Section IV intro) (pp. 247–254)
- Leverage: get other people and businesses to advertise for you. Four lead getters: customers (referrals), employees, agencies, affiliates/partners.

## Ch 15. #1 Customer Referrals: Word of Mouth (pp. 255–283)
- Referrals lower CAC and raise LTGP at once; growth from referrals compounds as long as more come in than churn out.
- Two reasons there are no referrals: the product isn't good enough to be worth mentioning ("why would customers be embarrassed to tell people?", p. 264), or you never ask.
- Goodwill is the gap between price and value (p. 265). Grow it by adding value, not cutting price.
- Six ways to build goodwill, mapped to ad parts (p. 266): sell to better-fit customers; set better expectations (under-promise until close rate starts to drop, then stop, p. 268); get more people the best results (survey, interview top customers, find their shared actions, make everyone do them, tie guarantee conditions to those actions, p. 270); deliver faster and more frequent wins (many small wins early, regular updates, always book the next meeting from the current one, p. 272); keep fixing the most common problem on a regular cycle (p. 273); tell them what to buy next after each win.
- Seven ways to ask (pp. 276–281): one-sided reward (pay the referrer your normal CAC); two-sided reward (split it between referrer and friend); ask at the moment of purchase ("people who do this with someone get better results — who would you bring?", p. 278); referrals as a negotiation chip (a discount in exchange for introductions); time-limited referral events; ongoing mentions of doing it with friends in all content; unlockable bonuses for referring plus a testimonial.
- Gift-card tactic (p. 280): give customers a card worth part of the price to pass to a named friend, with a short expiry.

## Ch 16. #2 Employees (pp. 284–301)
- Employees are lead getters; recruit them with the same Core Four (network, job posts, recruiting outreach, promoted posts, referrals).
- Train with the "3 Ds": document the steps as a checklist, demonstrate, then duplicate. If they get confused, the checklist is wrong. If qualified leads don't buy, it's a sales issue, not an ad issue.

## Ch 17. #3 Agencies (pp. 302–315)
- Use agencies to learn a new platform or method, not forever: hire one for a period, pay extra for them to explain their decisions, train your team, then drop to consulting.
- Start good-enough, then elite. Good signs: referrals, a waitlist, realistic expectations, no "hacks", simple regular reporting.

## Ch 18. #4 Affiliates and Partners (pp. 316–349)
- An affiliate is any business that already has your ideal customers and tells them about you. Results often lag weeks before affiliates activate.
- Find them by asking who already has my leads: what else they buy, where they go, which businesses serve them or employ them (software, products, equipment, services, groups, events).
- The offer to affiliates: a fast, simple way to earn more from customers they already have, with high likelihood and no building, delivering or support on their side. Call them out by owner type, their customers, their promised results, and ask your own customers who they know.
- Qualify by investment: make them a customer, or charge for training/certification; adjust the barrier if too few start or too few follow through.
- Pay for what you value (customers first, later leads or appointments). Work out the maximum you can pay per customer while keeping 3:1. Tiered payouts for signing up, activating, and staying.
- Launch with "whisper, tease, shout" (pp. 334–336): whisper builds curiosity well ahead; tease reveals value elements and the date; shout is the CTA with bonuses, scarcity and urgency at increasing frequency near the deadline. Do the work for affiliates (write their posts and emails).
- Three integration models: affiliate gives your lead magnet free with their own purchase; affiliate sells your lead magnet and keeps the cash; affiliate sells your core offer and you split revenue (pay ongoing, never cap).
- Start with 10–20 affiliates, fix problems, then use their results as the pitch to recruit more.

## Ch 19. Section IV Conclusion: Get Lead Getters (pp. 350–352)
- Expect a few months to make any new lead source work.

## Ch 20. Section V: Get Started / Open To Goal (pp. 353–368)
- Reserve a portion of budget for testing and keep following up engaged leads (e.g. text reminders) rather than letting them go cold.
- Volume beats perfection: a response rate that looks small (well under 1%) can be profitable at scale; test small before mass runs.
- "Open to goal" (p. 363): instead of doing a fixed number of actions, keep going each day until the outcome target is hit. Habit-stack it into a fixed time.
- One-page advertising checklist (p. 366): pick the type of engaged lead (customers, affiliates, employees, agencies); choose Rule of 100 or open to goal; fill in the daily action; do it until you can pay someone else; then make employees the next target and repeat.

## Ch 21. The Roadmap: Putting It All Together (pp. 369–375)
- Stage path: one offer, one avatar, one platform with warm outreach; add content; hire to run ads profitably; build a product good enough that a quarter or more of customers come from referrals; then add agencies and affiliates and scale.

## Ch 22. A Decade in a Page (pp. 376–381)
- Recap: engaged leads from the Core Four, multiplied by lead getters, improved by More-Better-New, driven daily by Rule of 100 or open to goal. Keep "rolling the dice" — repeated attempts beat waiting for a perfect plan.

## How to apply
(Invented illustrations across different kinds of business; none describes a real product.)
- Reveal-a-problem lead magnet: a bookkeeping app offers a free "How much are late invoices costing you?" calculator. It answers that question completely and shows the next problem (chasing payments by hand), which the paid product solves.
- One-step lead magnet: an interior designer offers a free one-room layout sketch from three photos. It is real work people would otherwise pay for, and it leads naturally into a quote.
- Callout plus value in titles and openings: "New to strength training? A 12-week plan you can do at home in 30 minutes" names the person, the outcome and the effort. Keep promises a notch below what the product delivers so reviews say "better than expected".
- Hooks with news elements: a coffee roaster posts "This week's harvest just landed: 3 beans we cupped twice" (recency, ongoing); a fitness coach posts "What I told a client who hadn't run in 10 years" (story, conflict); a numbered list such as "5 desk stretches that take under a minute". Mostly pure-give posts, an occasional ask.
- Marketplace to direct: after each sale through a marketplace, ask for a review with clear instructions and a "helps people like you" reason; where the platform's rules allow, offer a useful extra that brings the customer onto your own email list.
- Cold, personalised outreach: a freelance editor writes to a blog with one or two specific facts about a recent post, a concrete compliment and a fast piece of value (three fixed typos and a tighter intro). No meeting request; under half a page; one follow-up with a different angle.
- Reactivation: one line, no link, weeks later: "Are you still looking for a wedding photographer for June?"
- Affiliates and partners: a language school, a coworking space or a niche newsletter already has the audience. Give them a simple referral reward or a free resource for their members, and write their blurb for them.
- Measurement: count engaged leads (enquiries, downloads, replies, trials) per channel each week and change one thing at the step with the biggest drop-off.
