# Copywriting

One playbook for every piece of selling copy, whatever is being sold: a product, a service, an
app, a listing, a newsletter, a course, a personal brand. It distills techniques from several
copywriting and marketing books into one working method. The book notes live in `references/`;
this file is the method. It is deliberately generic: the facts, voice and audience of a specific
project come from that project's brief (see below), never from this file.

**If a project brief exists, load it.** Briefs live in `projects/<project>.md` (how they work and a
blank template: `projects/README.md`). Apply this method, then the brief on top: the brief's facts,
voice and banned words win over the generic defaults here.

Read the references as you need them. They hold the detail and page refs so this file can stay short.

## Ground rules (these override anything below)

- Nothing is published, sent or changed on a live page without the owner's approval of the exact text.
- Only true claims. Every number, inclusion, time, rating and policy comes from the live page, the
  team or the owner. If it is not confirmed, write `[confirm]` in the draft. Never invent reviews,
  scarcity, awards or guarantees.
- A brand's own voice rules (its voice card or project brief) win over generic advice.
- Platform rules win over copy tactics (character limits, banned words, no contact details on some
  marketplaces, review-request policies, ad policies).

## Core principles

1. **It is about the reader, not you.** People read about themselves. Open with their situation or
   gain, not your feelings, history or "Welcome to". Aim for roughly three "you" for every "we". [WTS] [SB] [OW]
2. **One reader, one goal, one offer.** Write to one typical buyer you can picture (their worry at
   3am, what they want more and less of). Each piece has one job, one main promise and one call to
   action. Fewer choices sell more. [WTS] [SB] [SUG]
3. **Sell what it does, not what it is.** Push every feature through "so what?" until it becomes a
   consequence the reader cares about. Automatic bank sync → no copying numbers by hand → your
   books are done in ten minutes on a Friday instead of a lost Sunday. [WTS] [SB]
4. **Emotion sells, logic justifies.** Lead with the experience and how it will feel; follow with the
   facts that let them defend the decision (specs, inclusions, price per unit, reviews). [SUG] [WTS]
5. **Specific beats general.** Exact names, numbers, times and places read as true and expert;
   "premium quality" reads as filler. Ask of every line: could this be more specific? [OW] [SUG] [WTS]
6. **Every line earns the next.** The headline gets the first sentence read; the first sentence is
   short and easy and gets the second read; each sentence must add new information. [SUG] [OW]
7. **Answer objections in the copy.** List every reason someone would hesitate and answer the
   biggest one early and plainly. A missing fact is a reason to wait. [SUG] [SB] [OFF]
8. **Proof everywhere.** Real reviews with names and dates, counts, credentials, original photos.
   Stock photos and hype adjectives signal "not true". [WTS] [SB] [OW] [SUG]
9. **Value before price.** Make the offer worth more (outcome, certainty, speed, ease) before
   touching the price. Never fix a weak offer by discounting alone. [OFF]
10. **Clear, not clever.** Plain words, active verbs, short sentences. No puns in titles that people
    search for. [LEADS] [WTS] [SB]

## The process (use for any piece)

1. **Get the facts.** Read the live page or brief (and `projects/<project>.md` if there is one),
   reviews, competitor pages and any data (search queries, sales, sign-ups). Note real customer
   phrases from reviews and support messages: they are pre-approved language. [GW] [SUG]
2. **Plan on one page** [WTS]:
   - Goal: the measurable action (a purchase, a booking, an enquiry, a reply, a download, a sign-up).
   - Reader: one sentence on who they are and what they worry about.
   - KFC: what must they **Know**, what should they **Feel**, what will they **Commit** to doing?
   - The one promise, in one sentence. If it will not fit in about twelve words, the thinking is not done. [OW]
   - Objections list (see Offer design for the four types).
3. **Find the angle.** Restate the task several ways, list several big ideas, pick one. Look for one
   distinctive true detail (from staff, customers, reviews, the founder) to build on. [SUG]
4. **Write the headline first**, then 10+ alternatives; pick by the headline tests below. [WTS] [OW]
5. **Draft fast** in the structure for the format (AIDCA for anything persuasive). Don't edit while drafting. [SUG] [WTS]
6. **Rest, then edit in passes**: structure → cut 30–50% → tone and voice → specificity → proofread.
   Read it aloud. [WTS] [SUG] [GW]
7. **Check against the plan and the facts.** Does it deliver the headline's promise? Is every claim
   confirmed? Then hand over the draft with short notes on what changed and why. [GW]
8. **After it goes live, test one thing at a time** at the biggest drop-off: no clicks → fix the
   headline, title or image; clicks but no action → fix the body, the offer, the price or the match
   between promise and page. [SB] [LEADS]

## Structure: AIDCA [WTS]

The default skeleton for persuasive copy, long or short:
- **Attention**: headline (and image). Benefit first; news or curiosity if you have it.
- **Interest**: the reader's situation, a picture of their day, the problem and the turn.
- **Desire**: benefits, vivid moments, what makes this different.
- **Conviction**: proof (reviews, numbers, credentials), objections answered, guarantee, price justified.
- **Action**: one clear, specific next step with a real reason to act now, repeated if the piece is long.

Supporting moves:
- **Slippery slide**: short opening line, momentum in the first two paragraphs, facts after the hook;
  end slower paragraphs with a short pull line ("Here is the part most people miss."). Use sparingly. [SUG]
- **Next-question order**: put each answer exactly where the reader would ask it. [SUG]
- **Story openers**: a real customer moment or a how-we-built-this story holds attention and sells. [SUG] [OW]
- **Problem chain, then solution chain**: problem → why → consequence → worst case; then solution → why it works → benefit → best case. [GW]
- **1/3/1 rhythm** for intros and paragraphs: one-line opener, three supporting lines, one-line close. [OW] [GW]
- **Subheads as full statements** so a skimmer gets the whole sell from the subheads alone. [OW]

## Headlines and titles

Tests every headline must pass:
- Says what it is about, who it is for, and the promise (outcome or problem solved). [OW]
- Benefit first; news and curiosity add power; all three together is strongest. [SB] [WTS]
- About 10 words, 16 at most, one line; overflow goes into a subhead or subtitle. [SB] [WTS]
- Uses the words the buyer types (for search, marketplace and app store titles, the exact phrase first). [SB] [LEADS]
- No full stop, no "Welcome to", no pun the reader must decode. [WTS] [SB]

Formulas:
- **Emergency headline**: finish "This helps my customers because it…", cut everything up to "it",
  change to "you" (and optionally start with "Now:" or "An easy way to"). [SB]
- **How to [get outcome] [without pain/in time frame]**. Prefer "how to" phrasing for lead magnets and guides. [LEADS] [WTS]
- **Number + specific thing + who + vivid promise**: "7 invoicing mistakes that delay freelancers' payments by a month". [OW]
- **Two-part title**: main promise + a specific subtitle adding proof or a second promise. [OW]
- **Callout**: a person label or a yes-question ("New to running?", "Still doing payroll in a spreadsheet?"). [LEADS]
- **News elements**: stack two of recency, relevance, proximity, conflict, unusual, ongoing. [LEADS]
- **Name an offer with MAGIC**: reason why, avatar (who), goal, time frame, container word
  ("The 6-Week Bread Basics Course for Busy Beginners"). [OFF]
- **Linking**: explain the new through the familiar ("like a personal trainer for your budget"). [SUG]

Rewrite a headline in four passes: say it fully; enlarge the promise; swap vague words for the
reader's own words; cut filler. [OW]

## Benefits vs features

A feature is what it is. A benefit is what it does for the reader. Run each feature through
"so what?" two or three times, in a two-column list, then write the benefit and keep the feature as proof. [WTS] [SB]

| Feature (proof) | Benefit (lead with this) |
|---|---|
| Bank sync for 3,000 banks (bookkeeping software) | Your books update themselves; no copying numbers on a Sunday |
| Roasted the week it ships (coffee subscription) | Every bag tastes like the first one; nothing stale in the cupboard |
| Evening and Saturday appointments (dental practice) | Get the tooth fixed without taking a day off work |
| Works offline (mobile app) | Use it on the plane or the subway, not only on Wi-Fi |
| 20 years in the industry (consultant) | Someone who has seen your problem before and knows which fixes last |
| Lifetime access, 40 short lessons (online course) | Learn in 10-minute gaps, at your pace, and come back when you need a refresher |
| Aluminium frame, 2-year warranty (physical product) | Light enough to carry upstairs; if it breaks, it's replaced |

Cover three levels where you can: the immediate practical gain, the basic human need (ease,
safety, status, connection), and the bigger meaning (the story they will tell, the person they
become). [WTS] [GW] Dramatize a hard-to-explain feature as a scene from the buyer's day. [SUG]
Write as if they have already bought ("When the first bag lands on your doormat on Monday..."),
with sensory detail. [SUG] [WTS]

## Offer design [OFF]

Copy cannot rescue a weak offer. Before polishing words, make the offer worth more.

**Value equation.** Perceived value rises with the *dream outcome* and the *certainty* of getting
it, and falls with the *time it takes* and the *effort* it demands. The cheapest wins are usually
in the bottom two: faster, easier. Answer the buyer's four silent questions in the copy: what do I
get, how sure is it, how fast, how much effort.

**Build the offer in five steps:**
1. **List every problem** the buyer hits before, during and after (for an online course: "what if
   I fall behind?", "do I need experience?", "will I actually finish?", "what if it's not for me?").
2. **Tag each as one of four objections**: not worth it, won't work for me, too hard, too slow.
3. **Turn each into a "how to" solution** line (how to finish even with a full-time job; how to
   start with zero experience). These become feature bullets and FAQ answers.
4. **Trim and stack.** Drop low-value items; keep cheap, high-value assets (checklists, templates,
   shortlists); present the rest as named parts, each with the problem it solves.
5. **Name it** with the MAGIC formula, and name each bonus the same way.

**Price.** Raise value before price. A premium price needs visible proof of a premium delivery
and a stated reason. Show price in the unit buyers compare (per month vs per year, per seat, per
person vs per group, per cup for a coffee subscription). [OFF] [SB] [SUG]

**Bonuses over discounts.** Each bonus answers one objection or solves the next problem, has a
benefit name and, where honest, a stated value. Tools beat more content. A complementary business
can supply a perk. [OFF]

**Guarantees.** Formula: if you don't get X by Y, we will Z, plus a reason why. Name the biggest
fear and reverse it; give the guarantee a vivid name instead of "satisfaction guaranteed". The more
generous it is, the more believable the business. Only offer what the business can honor. [OFF] [SUG]

**Scarcity and urgency, honestly.** Real capacity (seats in a cohort, appointment slots, units in
stock), real seasons (holiday gifting, tax deadlines, back-to-school), real price or bonus
deadlines. Put the deadline on the bonus or price, not on the product. Fake countdowns destroy
trust. [OFF] [WTS] [SUG]

**One main offer.** Show the core product first; offer add-ons (setup session, extra seats,
extended warranty) right after the first yes. [SUG] [SB]

Illustrations (invented): a coffee subscription could present "The Fresh-Roast Club" as the core,
with a "Brew Guide Card" and a "Skip or Swap Anytime" promise as low-cost bonuses that answer
"what if I don't like it?". A freelance consultant could package a "90-Minute Pricing Audit" +
"Rate Card Template" + "30 Days of Email Questions" as one named service with a clear guarantee.
See the offer notes for more.

## Lead generation and outreach [LEADS]

The goal of most marketing copy is a raised hand (an enquiry, a reply, a download, a sign-up),
counted every week per channel.

**Lead magnets.** Solve one narrow problem completely, in a way that reveals the next problem the
paid product solves. Types: reveal a problem (calculator, audit, checklist, quiz), a sample or
trial, or one step of a multi-step process. Spend most effort on the name; "how to" names work.
Make it good enough to charge for. Examples: a pricing calculator for a SaaS tool, a free first
lesson for a course, a "which roast suits you" quiz for a coffee brand, a home-office checklist
for a furniture shop.

**Calls to action.** Exact action + a reason to act now (real scarcity, real urgency, or a plain
"because"). [LEADS] [WTS]

**Warm outreach** (past customers, partners, friends of the business): a personal opener with a
real reason, up to three touches, then acknowledge, compliment, ask. Ask "do you know anyone
who...?" rather than pitching. Reactivate cold contacts with one short line and no link ("Still
looking for a bookkeeper?"). [LEADS]

**Cold outreach** (bloggers, partners, publications, prospects): one or two specific facts about
*their* page or business, a concrete compliment, then big, fast value (a fixed figure, a tool, a
suggested paragraph, a quick audit). No meeting request on first contact. Under half a page. Judge
only after real volume; rough benchmark ~30% open × ~10% reply. Follow up once with a new angle.
[LEADS] [GW] [SB]

**Content and social hooks.** Each post is hook → retain → reward, and the reward delivers exactly
what the hook promised. Retain with numbered lists, steps or a story. Give far more than you ask;
put the occasional ask at the most valuable moment. Topics: customer stories, what changed this
week, seasonal timing, mistakes beginners make, behind the scenes. [LEADS] [OW]

**Referrals and partners.** Ask at the moment of purchase or right after a win, framed as a
better experience with friends. Two-sided rewards. Partners (adjacent businesses, communities,
newsletters, agencies) already have the audience: give them an easy reason and write their blurb
for them. [LEADS]

**Paid ads** (if used): callout → value → call to action, most effort on the first seconds; the
landing page matches the ad's promise exactly. [LEADS] [SB]

## Voice: brand and founder [GW]

- Write a **voice card** per brand: five to eight attributes (word choice, sentence length, rhythm,
  structure, use of data, humor, what it never does). Check every draft against it. If a project
  brief exists in `projects/`, its voice section is the voice card.
- **Audit first.** Gather everything the founder or brand has already written or said (posts,
  replies to customers, emails, reviews they liked); reuse their phrases.
- **Record and transcribe** for founder-voice pieces: a 20-minute call with four prompts (the first
  time you..., your take on..., where is this going..., walk a beginner through it). Edit the
  transcript: cut small talk and tangents, find the theme, set headline and subheads, trim.
- **Content first, voice last.** Get the argument right, then tune the voice.
- Default to a **declarative** voice (direct, plain, confident). Pick one archetype per piece:
  storyteller, opinionator, fact presenter, frameworker.
- To sound factual: remove emphasis adverbs, add one sourced figure per section ("According to the
  national statistics office..."). To sound opinionated: firm picks, fewer hedges.
- When someone says "this doesn't sound like me", go line by line and have them point; change the
  fewest things. Never make them sound smarter than they are; keep their natural phrasing.
- Never reuse one brand's distinctive phrasing for another.

## Templates by format

Fill the brackets from real facts only. Anything you cannot verify goes in as [confirm], never as
a guess. Check the platform's current character limits before you finalise; the numbers below are
common defaults, not guarantees.

### 1. Marketplace or directory listing (tours, products, rentals, apps, services)

Buyers scan, compare several listings side by side, and decide on price, reviews, images and the
first two lines. The title and the opening do most of the work; the rest answers objections.

**Title** (keep the key phrase in the first ~50 characters)
- Pattern A: `[What it is] for [who] – [main differentiator]`
- Pattern B: `[Place or category]: [Format] [Thing] with [Key inclusion]`
- Pattern C: `[Brand-free description] | [Spec that buyers filter on] | [Proof or guarantee]`
- Use the words buyers type and filter on (category, size, location, format, the key feature).
  Look at the top 5 titles for the same search first.
- No hype adjectives in titles ("amazing", "unforgettable"). No brand name unless it is known.

**Summary / first 2 lines** (shown on the card or above the fold; ~200–300 characters)
- Sentence 1: what you get, in concrete terms (what, how much, how long, from where).
- Sentence 2: the 3–4 strongest specifics, then the flexibility or risk-reducing promise.
- Do not open with "Enjoy", "Discover", "Experience". Open with the reader or the thing.

**Highlights** (4–6 bullets, each one a benefit with its proof)
1. Convenience benefit: what effort it removes (delivery, set-up, pick-up, booking)
2. Fit benefit: who it is for, size or capacity, language or compatibility
3. Flexibility: what can be changed and how (sizes, dates, add-ons, cancellation)
4. One or two vivid, specific moments or details (a thing + an action)
5. Risk reducer or proof: returns or cancellation window, rating and review count
- One idea per bullet. No two bullets saying the same thing. Proofread every one.

**Full description** (short paragraphs, scannable)
1. Problem → turn: what it is like the hard way, then how this removes that.
2. What happens or what you get, in order, one line of "why it matters" each.
3. Who it suits (and, honestly, who it does not).
4. Options and upgrades, with prices if fixed.
5. Practical: delivery or location, timing, policies, what to bring or what you need.

**Inclusions / exclusions**
- List what is included first and plainly [confirm each].
- Then a separate, short "Not included".
- Platforms sometimes render both lists together. Read the live page as a buyer would.

**Checklist before publishing a listing**
- Title contains the search keyword and the differentiator
- First two lines make sense with nothing else visible
- Price unit is right (per item, per person, per group, per night, per month) and matches the options
- Duration, size or spec fields match the description
- Every claim is true today
- Same core promise in title, summary, highlights and description
- No typos (read it aloud once)

### 2. Landing page or homepage section

One section, one job. Order:
1. Headline: the outcome for the reader, specific (number, time, result) or a sharp question.
2. Sub-head: who it is for and how it works, in one line.
3. Body: 2–4 short paragraphs or 3–5 bullets turning features into consequences.
4. Proof: a real review line with name and date, a count, a credential, a press mention.
5. Objection killer: price clarity, cancellation, what happens if X.
6. One call to action with the next step stated ("Start free for 14 days", "Book a 15-minute call" [confirm the promise]).
For multilingual pages, write each language natively. Do not translate headlines word for word; keep the promise, change the idiom.

### 3. Sales page (long form)

1. Headline + sub-head: the big promise and who it is for.
2. Lead: the reader's situation and problem, told as a scene or a short story (problem chain).
3. The turn: what changed, why the usual fixes fail, what makes this different (solution chain).
4. The offer: core product, then the named stack of bonuses, each with the problem it solves.
5. Proof: testimonials with specifics, numbers, case studies, credentials.
6. Price with a reason, framed against the alternatives.
7. Guarantee with a name, then FAQ answering the remaining objections.
8. Call to action, repeated, with a real reason to act now; a P.S. restating the main benefit.

### 4. Product description (e-commerce)

- Line 1: the outcome or moment of use, not the material ("Coffee that's still hot at your 11am meeting").
- 3–5 bullets: benefit first, spec as proof ("Keeps drinks hot for 12 hours – double-wall steel").
- One short paragraph of sensory or use detail, written as if they already own it.
- Specs block for the logical buyer: size, weight, materials, care, compatibility.
- Risk reducer: shipping time, returns, warranty [confirm each].

### 5. Cold or warm outreach email (partners, publishers, prospects, press)

- Subject line: specific to them, under ~50 characters, no clickbait.
  e.g. "A fix for the pricing table on your guide" / "Free calculator for your readers"
- Line 1: about them (the exact article, page or problem you noticed). Not about you.
- Line 2–3: the useful thing you offer and why it helps them or their audience.
- Line 4: credibility in one clause (credential, years, real data, a known client).
- Ask: one small, easy yes ("Would it be useful if I sent...?" / "Who is best to send this to?").
- Sign-off with a real name and role. No attachments on first contact unless asked.
- Under 150 words. Read it as the busiest person on their team would.
- Never send without the owner's approval of the exact text.

### 6. App store text

- **App name / subtitle (30 characters each on the App Store):** subtitle = the job the app does,
  in the words people search ("Track habits in 10 seconds" style), not a slogan.
- **Promotional text (170 characters, editable any time):** the newest reason to download now
  (an update, a season, a new count of items), benefit first.
- **Description opening (first 3 lines show before "more"):** the problem, the outcome, the proof.
  Then short benefit bullets, then features, then languages, privacy and support.
- Keep counts and claims in sync with the app (number of items, languages, platforms).

### 7. Social post (Instagram, Facebook, X, Threads, LinkedIn)

- Hook line: one concrete, surprising fact or a tension; it must work alone in the preview.
- Body: 2–4 short paragraphs, one idea each, numbers over adjectives.
- Practical line (where, when, cost) if it is a place, product or event.
- One honest tie-in to the business and one call to action.

### 8. Ad (search, social, display)

- Search ad: the searcher's exact phrase in the headline, one concrete benefit, one proof point, one action. [SB]
- Social or video ad: callout in the first line or first seconds, then value, then the action. [LEADS]
- The landing page repeats the ad's promise in its headline. One ad, one promise.

### 9. Newsletter and subject lines

- Subject line: a mini headline. Specific, under ~50 characters, curiosity or benefit, never a bait-and-switch.
- Preview text: completes the subject line, it does not repeat it.
- Body: useful content first (roughly three quarters or more), promotion last and short. [SB]
- One main link or call to action per issue; a P.S. for a second, smaller one.

### 10. Bio / about page

- Lead with who you help and the result, not your birth year or founding date.
- Then the credibility: one origin moment, specific numbers, credentials, notable clients.
- Then what you believe or do differently, in plain words, and one human detail.
- End with what to do next (book, subscribe, get in touch). Third person for directories, first person for your own site.

## Persuasion triggers to check [SUG] [WTS]

A quick review list, drawn mainly from Sugarman's psychological triggers. Use the ones that are true for the product; never fake them.
- **Ownership**: the reader imagines already using it ("when you open the dashboard on Monday...").
- **Honesty**: state one real limit up front and why it is minor (e.g. "it takes two weeks to arrive because we roast to order").
- **Authority**: one consistent line of expertise (years in practice, credentials, number of customers).
- **Proof of value**: compare with the alternatives (doing it yourself, hiring someone, the usual product), break the price down per use.
- **Reason why**: explain any unusual price, high or low.
- **Satisfaction conviction**: a policy so generous it signals confidence; place it near the end.
- **Linking**: tie the new thing to something familiar.
- **Belonging**: show who already buys (with real reviews from people like the reader).
- **Curiosity**: hold one thing back so the reader clicks or reads on.
- **Timing**: tie to the season or what is in the news, only when true.
- Deeper motives, used subtly: pride, envy, the comfort of ease (laziness is a selling point), annoyance at the alternative. [WTS]

## Editing checklist

Run in this order. Rest the draft first if you can. [WTS] [SUG] [GW]

Plan and promise
- [ ] Does the piece deliver exactly what the headline promises?
- [ ] One goal, one main offer, one call to action?
- [ ] Is the reader's gain in the first two lines?

Cut
- [ ] Delete the first paragraph and see if it reads better (it usually does). [SB]
- [ ] Cut 30–50% of the first draft; ask of each sentence "is there a simpler way?" [SUG]
- [ ] Every sentence adds new information; delete restatements. [OW]
- [ ] Remove warm-up phrases ("It is important to note that", "In order to", "We are pleased to").

Words
- [ ] No hype words: amazing, unique, breathtaking, unforgettable, world-class, best-in-class, solutions. Replace each with the reason it is exciting. [SB] [WTS]
- [ ] Short, plain words over long ones (buy, not purchase; start, not commence). [SB] [WTS]
- [ ] Verbs, not nouns ("decide", not "make a decision"). Active voice: search for "by" and "to be". [WTS]
- [ ] No filler openers: Enjoy, Discover, Experience, Welcome to.
- [ ] About three "you" per "we".

Rhythm and readability
- [ ] Average sentence ~10 words for email and mobile, ~12–16 for web; mix in very short ones. [SB] [WTS]
- [ ] Paragraphs of 2–3 lines on screen; never three dense paragraphs in a row. [WTS] [OW]
- [ ] Readability around grade 7–8 (Flesch Reading Ease 60+). [WTS] [SUG]
- [ ] Subheads alone tell the story; key sentences can be bolded for skimmers. [OW]

Truth and proof
- [ ] Every fact checked against the live source; anything unverified marked [confirm].
- [ ] Reviews quoted are real, attributed, and allowed by the platform.
- [ ] Biggest objection answered; price unit clear; inclusions and exclusions clear.

Voice and final pass
- [ ] Matches the brand voice card; would the founder actually say each line? Read it aloud. [GW] [SB]
- [ ] Proofread separately, after a gap, one thing at a time (names, numbers, then spelling). Spellcheck misses real-word errors. [WTS]
- [ ] Platform limits met (characters, banned content, no contact details where not allowed).

## Common mistakes

- Opening with the company ("Founded in 2009, we...", "We are delighted to...") instead of the reader. [WTS] [SB]
- A list of features or names presented as the selling point; features with no consequence. [WTS]
- Category words ("innovative solutions", "premium quality") where one concrete image would do. [OW]
- Two promises that contradict ("cancel anytime" in the headline, "12-month minimum" in the terms). [GW]
- Hiding the strongest facts (rating, price, what is included) below the fold. [SUG]
- Wrong price unit or a duration field that contradicts the description; these kill trust instantly.
- Stock photos and generic captions; captions are some of the most-read copy. [SUG] [WTS] [SB]
- Fake urgency or invented scarcity. [WTS] [OFF] [SUG]
- Too many options or calls to action in one piece. [SUG] [SB]
- Clever titles and puns where people search. [SB] [LEADS]
- Selling the upgrade before the core product. [SUG] [OFF]
- Typos in titles and highlights. [WTS] [SUG]
- First-contact outreach that asks for a call or talks about yourself. [LEADS] [GW]
- Humor that does not fit the product, and polishing copy for a product buyers do not want; fix the offer first. [SUG]
- Translating a headline word for word between languages instead of rewriting it natively.

## Adding a new source

This skill is built to absorb more books and courses.
1. Read the whole source and write `references/<short-name>-notes.md` in the same shape as the
   others: a title line, one section per chapter with page refs, then "Techniques to merge" and
   "How to apply" (with invented, varied examples, never one real business). Paraphrase only; quotes no longer than a short named phrase.
2. Give it a source tag and add it to the tag list and the reference table at the top of this file.
3. Merge only techniques that add something new or sharpen an existing rule; tag them. When two
   sources disagree, keep both with tags and say which fits which format.
4. Add or update an example in `examples/` if the new source changes how a format is written.

## Adding a project

Keep this file generic. When copy is needed for a new project, copy the template in
`projects/README.md` to `projects/<project>.md` and fill it from verified facts only (anything
unverified marked [confirm]). Project facts, voice rules, names and numbers live there, never here.
