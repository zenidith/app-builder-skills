# Linking to existing sites, and the red flags in detail

Sources are tagged; untagged rules are this skill's working defaults.

## Linking a new site into a family of sites

Owners with several sites often want a new one to send readers (and link value) to the others.
Done well, it helps readers; done badly, it looks like a link scheme.

**Do**
- Link from content where the reader benefits: a news item about a rule change linking to the sister
  site's practical guide; a guide linking to the sister site's tool, app or booking page.
- Use natural anchor text that describes the destination, and vary it.
- Name the family of sites on each about page ("part of the same small group as ...").
- Give each site its own purpose, content and audience, so it would be worth visiting alone.
- Cross-promote through channels too: newsletter mentions, social posts, in-app links, shared
  events, co-branded resources.
- Label paid and affiliate links (sponsored or nofollow), including links to your own businesses
  when a reader could see them as advertising.

**Don't**
- Put sitewide footer or sidebar blocks linking every site to every other site.
- Swap links for their own sake or build "partner" pages whose only job is cross-linking; Google's
  spam policies list excessive link exchanges among link schemes. [GS]
- Build thin sites (or buy expired domains) mainly to pass links to a money site; that is a private
  blog network and expired-domain abuse territory. [GS]
- Repeat the same exact-match anchor text on every link.
- Copy the same articles across the family; each site writes its own.

**A quick test:** if search engines did not exist, would you still put this link here for the
reader? If yes, keep it.

## Red flags in detail

**YMYL ("your money or your life").** Topics that can affect health, finances, safety, legal
standing or civic understanding (including news on public affairs) are judged by a higher standard
of experience, expertise, authority and trust in Google's Quality Rater Guidelines. A new site
without credentials will struggle to rank there, and mistakes can harm readers. [QRG]

**Ad-restricted subjects.** Ad network policies ban some content outright and restrict ads on
other subjects such as sexual content, shocking or violent content, gambling, drugs, alcohol,
tobacco and weapons; many advertisers also block crime and tragedy stories for brand safety. The
result is fewer ads and lower revenue per view. Check the current policy pages before choosing an
edgy niche; they change. [PUB]

**One traffic source.** Search updates have cut many niche and news sites' traffic sharply in a
single round; social platforms change reach without notice; aggregators can drop a source. Plan a
second channel from day one (newsletter, social following, app, direct visitors, partners), and
track what share of visits each channel brings.

**Saturation.** A niche everyone recommends to beginners, with every long-tail angle covered,
fails the competition test even if readers spend heavily. [AM]

**Fragile inputs.** A model that depends on scraping one source that can block you or change its
terms; a domain built on someone else's trademark; a supply of stories that only exists for one
season or trend.

**Legal exposure.** Crime, celebrity and tabloid formulas carry copyright, defamation and privacy
risk; see `news-site-notes.md`.

**Owner capacity.** A daily site needs daily review. If nobody can give it a fixed slot each day,
choose an evergreen niche or a weekly format instead.
