# Anne Moss: how she picks a niche

Source: Anne Moss, guest interview on the Niche Pursuits podcast, published 14 Sep 2022, "How Anne
Moss Earns $200K Per Month From A Portfolio of 25 Sites & 5 Million Monthly Pageviews"
(https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VS64Qs4As6A). Timestamps are from the episode. Everything below is
a paraphrase, not a transcript. Figures (25 sites, 50 staff, monthly revenue) are as stated in the
episode in 2022 and are context only.

Context: she builds every site from scratch (no site purchases), earns mainly from display ads, and
publishes a large volume of informational articles through writing teams.

## The four tests (18:27–25:07)

### 1. Enough traffic in the niche (18:27)
- First check: are people actually searching for this subject?
- Decide the size up front. Sometimes she picks a big niche and a domain that could hold 5,000 to
  10,000 posts one day; sometimes a microniche where about 500 posts is the natural limit. Both
  have pros and cons; whichever she picks, the search volume must be there.

### 2. Competition (19:13)
- Before buying a domain she writes a content plan and does the topic research.
- Target: at least 50 topics that she expects, on average, to bring a few hundred page views a
  month each, judged on both search volume and competition.
- She looks for very low or zero competition: questions nobody has targeted directly yet.
- Decision rule: if she can find those 50 easily, within two to three hours, the niche goes ahead.
  If after about two hours she has barely 10 to 15, she skips it and looks for another niche.

### 3. Monetization potential (20:33)
- Display ads can go on any site, so the question is the likely RPM (revenue per 1,000 page
  views). She judges it by how the niche as a whole is monetized.
- If readers buy a lot (her example: dog owners buy supplies and pay large vet bills), advertisers
  will pay to reach them, so campaigns will target that audience.
- Sometimes it is about the audience rather than the topic: readers of a luxury travel site are
  likely to buy high-end products in general, so advertisers want them whatever the article is.

### 4. Ease of content creation (21:59)
- She calls this a smaller consideration, but it matters for cost and speed.
- Popular, well-loved subjects (dogs) make it easy to find writers who enjoy the work.
- Dry or technical subjects (finance, in her view) are harder to staff, and need real expertise
  before a writer can even start; a basic pet-care post needs far less background research.

### Three out of four can be enough (24:01)
- Nothing is perfect. She will go ahead with three strong tests out of four, for example when
  search volume is uncertain but competition is low, RPM looks high and writers will be easy to
  find. Not every site succeeds, and she accepts that.
- The host's summary: look for under-served or unserved topics, in a space where people spend
  money, that is easy to staff without paying a fortune for writers.

### Why she avoids the dog niche (25:07)
- Pets, and dogs especially, are saturated. Beginner courses tell people to write about what they
  love, everybody loves dogs (and fishing), and everybody uses the same long-tail research methods,
  so every angle is already covered. High spending does not help when there is no gap.

## Other useful points from the same episode

- **Tool scores mislead (about 40:10–41:30).** Her team uses several keyword tools (she names
  Ahrefs and LowFruits) and keeps trying new ones, but she does not decide whether a topic goes into
  the plan on tool numbers alone, because they can be very misleading. Many people chase the same
  batch of "low competition" terms the tools surface, which is part of why competition has risen.
- **Dollars per post (about 32:30).** She checks stats daily to catch problems early, and does a
  monthly review of the whole portfolio with her business partner. The main number is dollars per
  post: total site revenue divided by posts, which moves with either traffic or RPM, so a drop is a
  prompt to find out which.

## How to apply

- Run the four tests in this order: volume, competition, money, production. Competition is the one
  with a hard skip rule; the others can trade off.
- Treat the 50-topic list as both the test and the first content plan. Record for each topic the
  monthly searches, the difficulty, the tool and the date, and what ranks today.
- Time-box the research. The two-to-three-hour limit is part of the test: if gaps are that hard to
  find, they will not get easier at post 200.
- Avoid niches whose main appeal is "lots of people love it"; look for spending audiences with
  questions nobody has answered well.
- After launch, track dollars per post (or visits per post before ads are on) monthly per site.
