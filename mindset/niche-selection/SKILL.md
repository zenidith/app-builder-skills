# Niche selection

One method for deciding whether a niche is worth a domain, a content plan and months of work. It is
built around Anne Moss's four tests, then adds the research steps, a scoring sheet, a news-site
variant, linking rules and red flags from other well-known sources. It is deliberately generic: it
works for any topic, country or language. Facts about one owner's existing sites belong in a
project file (see `projects/README.md`), never here.

## Ground rules (these override anything below)

- Decide before you buy. The content plan and topic list come first; the domain comes last. [AM]
- Numbers are real or marked. Every search volume and difficulty figure names its tool and date;
  anything guessed is marked `[estimate]` and anything not looked up is marked `not checked`.
- A niche needs searches, room to rank, money behind the audience and a way to keep producing
  content. Three strong tests out of four can be enough; a failed competition test is not. [AM]
- No niche is worth copyright, defamation or search-spam risk. If the business model only works by
  rewriting other people's articles at scale, it is the wrong model (see "News site variant").
- Nothing is published, bought, sent or linked from a live site without the owner's yes.

## The process (use for any candidate)

1. **Write the candidate down in one line:** who the reader is, what they need, and why they would
   come back. "Owners of small aquarium shrimp who keep losing them" beats "aquariums".
2. **Size it.** Big niche (room for thousands of posts, built over years) or microniche (a few
   hundred posts, then maintain)? Both are valid; the choice sets the domain name and the plan. [AM]
3. **Run the topic test.** Spend 2–3 hours building a list of low- or zero-competition topics with
   real searches (method below). 50 found easily = go on. Barely 10–15 after two hours = skip. [AM]
4. **Check the money.** How is this industry monetized as a whole? Do readers spend? Would
   advertisers pay to reach them? Note affiliate programs, sponsors and any business of your own
   the site can feed. [AM]
5. **Check production.** Can you find writers or sources who know the subject and enjoy it? How
   much expertise does a first useful post need? [AM]
6. **Check the red flags** (YMYL, ad-restricted subjects, one traffic source, saturation, legal).
7. **Score it** on the sheet (below). Compare candidates side by side, never one in isolation.
8. **Decide:** go, small test, or no-go. For a go, keep the topic list: it is the first content plan.

## The four tests [AM]

### 1. Search volume
Enough people must already be searching for the subject. Moss's bar: each of the first 50 topics
should, on average, be able to bring a few hundred visits a month. Decide early whether you are
building a big site (a domain broad enough for 5,000–10,000 posts) or a microniche capped near 500
posts; a microniche is fine if you know it is one.

Check: total monthly searches across the topic list, how many terms have real numbers (not "-"),
seasonality (look at 12 months, not one), and whether the subject keeps generating new questions.

### 2. Competition
Before committing, do the topic research and try to list at least 50 topics where the results are
weak or nobody has written the exact answer yet. If those 50 come quickly (2–3 hours), go on. If
two hours of work produce only 10–15, skip the niche. Moss does not decide from tool difficulty
scores alone: she says they can mislead, so she reads the actual results (detail in
`references/topic-research-notes.md`). She also stays out of niches every beginner course points
people at (dogs, fishing): the spending is there, but every angle is already covered.

### 3. Monetization (ad RPM potential)
RPM is revenue per 1,000 page views. Judge it by how the whole niche makes money: lots of products,
big recurring bills (pet owners and vet bills is her example) mean advertisers will pay to reach
those readers. Sometimes it is the reader, not the topic: a luxury travel audience is worth a lot to
advertisers in general because it has money to spend. Moss tracks "dollars per post" across her
portfolio as the main health number.

Check: affiliate programs and their payouts, whether brands advertise around the subject, whether
the readers are buyers (planning, comparing, booking) or browsers, and ad restrictions (below).

### 4. Ease of content creation
Some subjects are easy to staff because many people know and love them; others need real expertise
just to write a first answer (finance is her example). A post that needs a professional to write it
costs more and goes slower; that is acceptable only if the other tests are strong.

Check: could a capable writer produce a correct post after an hour of research? Are there primary
sources (official data, manufacturers, organisers) to work from? Will the subject still interest
writers after 200 posts?

## Scoring sheet

Score each test 1–5 with a one-line reason. Copy `templates/scoring-sheet.md` (or the CSV) for a
real comparison.

| Score | Search volume | Competition | Monetization | Ease of content |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | 50+ topics with real numbers, room for 1,000+ posts | 50+ easy topics found in under 3 hours | Buyers with high spend; strong ads and affiliates | Many keen writers and primary sources |
| 4 | Clear demand, a solid microniche or a growing big niche | 30–50 easy topics found | Spending audience, some affiliate or sponsor fit | Writers available with light research |
| 3 | Demand exists but thin or seasonal | 15–30 easy topics; results mixed | Average ads, few affiliates | Needs research per post, sources exist |
| 2 | Few terms with numbers; mostly "-" | 10–15 easy topics after 2 hours | Low-spend readers or ad-restricted subject | Needs specialists or hard-to-reach sources |
| 1 | Almost nobody searches | Under 10; results owned by big brands | No clear money | Only experts can write it |

**Decision rules (working defaults):**
- **Go:** total 15 or more out of 20, competition 3 or more, no red flag that blocks ads or
  creates legal risk.
- **Small test:** total 12–14, or one test at 2 with the other three at 4 or more (Moss's "three
  out of four"). Publish 20–30 posts from the topic list and measure for 3–6 months before scaling.
- **No-go:** total under 12, competition 2 or less (her skip rule), or a blocking red flag.
- When two candidates tie, prefer the one with the longer easy-topic list and the fewer red flags.

Optional columns (do not change the total, but break ties): fit with the owner's existing sites,
owner's own knowledge or access, how much can be automated safely, size of the 12-month content
plan.

## Topic research, in short

Full method in `references/topic-research-notes.md`.

1. **Seed list.** Write 20–30 seed phrases from the reader's problems, not your product.
2. **Expand.** Run seeds through a keyword tool (Keysearch, Ahrefs, LowFruits or similar) for
   monthly searches and a difficulty score (0–100). Add Google autocomplete ("alphabet soup": the
   seed plus each letter) [IS], "People also ask", related searches, and the questions real people
   ask on Reddit, forums, Quora, Facebook groups and app reviews.
3. **Filter.** Keep terms with real searches (as a starting bar, 50+ a month for one term, or a
   cluster of close variants summing 100+) and a low difficulty (30 or less is a reasonable
   starting bar on a 0–100 scale). Mark "-" volume as "no data", not zero.
4. **Read the results page for every keeper.** A topic is truly easy when the top results include
   forum threads, Q&A pages, thin or old posts, or pages that answer a different question. It is
   hard when big brands and exact, recent, thorough answers fill the first page. Tool scores are a
   shortlist, the results page is the verdict. [AM]
5. **Cross-check the easy ones** with a quick ratio such as the Keyword Golden Ratio (pages with the
   exact phrase in their title, divided by monthly searches; under 0.25 for terms under ~250
   searches suggests a gap). [KGR]
6. **Count.** Stop at 50 (go on), or at two hours with 10–15 (skip). Keep the list: volume,
   difficulty, what ranks now, the post you would write.

## News site variant

Use when the idea is a daily news, alerts or curation site rather than evergreen guides. Full notes,
sources and the pipeline in `references/news-site-notes.md`.

- **Daily sources first.** List where tomorrow's stories come from: official press releases,
  government notices and open data, regulators, event and venue listings, company announcements,
  local-language press, community reports. A news niche with no steady primary sources is a
  rewriting niche.
- **Originality over rewriting.** The value must be something readers cannot get from the source:
  translation of local-language material into a clear summary with a link to the original, context,
  "what this means for you", data you collect, a calendar or tracker. Google asks whether content is
  made for people or made to rank, and its spam policies treat mass-produced pages with little added
  value as "scaled content abuse", however they were produced. [GS]
- **No paid "parasite" posts.** Selling space for third-party pages that ride the site's reputation
  (casino, loans, coupons) is "site reputation abuse" in Google's spam policies and can sink the
  whole site. [GS]
- **Legal checks.** Facts are free; someone's article is not. Translating a whole article is
  republishing it in another language and needs permission; summarise in your own words, quote
  briefly with credit and a link. Report arrests and allegations as allegations, from an official
  source, and correct quickly. Some countries treat defamation as a crime and do not accept truth
  alone as a defence. When in doubt, ask a lawyer before launch, not after a complaint.
- **A human review step.** In any automated pipeline (sources → collect → de-duplicate → draft
  summary → review → publish → share), a person approves every story before it goes live and checks
  names, numbers and claims against the source. Nothing about crime, health, money or named private
  people publishes automatically.
- **Trust signals.** Real bylines, an about page, a contact and corrections policy, and dates on
  every story. [QRG]

## Linking to existing sites

Detail in `references/linking-and-risk-notes.md`.

- Link where it helps the reader: a story that naturally points to a guide, tool or service on a
  sister site. One relevant link inside the content beats a block of links in every footer.
- Say who owns what: an about page line naming the family of sites is honest and reassuring.
- No link swaps for their own sake, no network of sites built only to pass links, no identical
  anchor text everywhere. Google's spam policies call out excessive link exchanges and partner
  pages made purely for cross-linking. [GS]
- Each site must stand on its own: unique content, its own audience, worth visiting without the
  others. Mark paid and affiliate links as such.
- Cross-promote through channels as well as links: newsletter mentions, social posts, in-app
  links, shared events.

## Red flags

- **YMYL ("your money or your life") topics:** health, finance, legal, safety, news on civic
  matters. They are held to a higher standard of expertise and trust; without real credentials
  they rank poorly and carry risk. [QRG]
- **Ad-restricted subjects:** sexual content, shocking or violent content, gambling, drugs, weapons
  and similar get fewer or no ads under ad network policies and advertiser brand-safety filters,
  which cuts RPM. Check the current rules before choosing an edgy niche. [PUB]
- **One traffic source:** a site that lives on one search engine, one social platform or one
  aggregator can lose most of its traffic in a single update. Plan a second channel (newsletter,
  social, app, direct audience) from the start.
- **Saturation:** the niche every course recommends, with every long-tail angle already covered. [AM]
- **Fragile inputs:** a content model that depends on scraping one source that can block you, or on
  a trademark in the domain.
- **Seasonality or a one-off trend** with nothing to write once it passes.

## Output format

For each candidate, deliver:

1. One-line definition (reader, need, why they return) and big niche or microniche.
2. The four scores with one-line reasons, the total, and go / small test / no-go.
3. Red flags found (or "none found").
4. For a go or small test: the topic list (aim for 50) with monthly searches, difficulty, the tool
   and date, what ranks now, and "not checked" where no data exists.
5. For a news site: the daily source list, the pipeline with its review step, and the legal checks.
6. Linking plan to existing sites, if any.

## Common mistakes

- Buying the domain first and researching afterwards. [AM]
- Choosing a niche because you love it, when everyone else did the same. [AM]
- Trusting a difficulty score without reading the results page. [AM]
- Treating "-" (no volume data) as zero, or a guessed number as a measured one.
- Counting on display ads in a subject advertisers avoid.
- Planning an automated site with no human review, no original angle and no named sources.
- Building a network of thin sites to link to a money site.
- Scoring one candidate alone; the sheet works when ideas compete.

## Adding a new source

1. Read or watch the whole source and write `references/<short-name>-notes.md` in the same shape as
   the others: a title line with the source and date, its key points in your own words with
   timestamps or page refs, then "How to apply". Paraphrase only.
2. Give it a source tag and add it to the tag list and the reference table at the top of this file.
3. Merge only rules that add something new or sharpen an existing one; tag them. When two sources
   disagree, keep both with tags and say which fits which kind of site.
